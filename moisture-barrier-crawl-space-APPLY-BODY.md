# Moisture Barrier for a Crawl Space

Bare soil under a house gives off water vapor constantly. In a crawl space, that moisture raises the humidity, feeds mold on wood framing, and rises into the living space above. A moisture barrier stops it at the source.

**A crawl space moisture barrier is a polyethylene sheet laid over the dirt floor so ground moisture can't evaporate into the crawl space. A basic 6-mil ground cover costs about $60 to $115 in material for 1,000 sq ft; a full encapsulation, with a heavier liner up the walls, sealed vents and usually a dehumidifier, runs into the thousands.**

## Why a crawl space needs one

The EPA recommends keeping indoor humidity between 30% and 50%, and below 60%, to keep mold from growing. A crawl space open to damp soil easily sits above that. Covering the soil removes the biggest moisture source, and air sealing and a dehumidifier handle the rest.

## Liner materials

Liner thickness is measured in mils, thousandths of an inch.

- **6-mil polyethylene:** the basic ground cover. It blocks vapor well but tears easily if people crawl on it.
- **10- to 12-mil reinforced polyethylene:** a woven reinforcement makes it much harder to puncture, a sensible minimum if anyone will work in the space.
- **20-mil liners:** used for full encapsulation, tough enough for regular foot traffic and for running up the walls.

All of these are Class I vapor retarders. For unvented crawl spaces, the International Residential Code (section R408.3) requires a continuous Class I vapor retarder over the exposed earth, with seams overlapped and sealed and the edges run up the walls.

## Ground cover vs. full encapsulation

- **Ground cover:** a liner over the dirt floor, seams taped. It's the cheapest step and blocks most ground moisture, while the vents stay as they are.
- **Full encapsulation:** a heavier liner over the floor and up the walls, sealed seams, sealed vents and usually a dehumidifier. It turns the crawl space into a dry, semi-conditioned space.

Fix any water coming in first. A liner over standing water or an active leak just hides the problem; drainage, gutters and grading come before the barrier.

## Humidity control after encapsulation

An encapsulated crawl space still needs its humidity controlled, usually with a dehumidifier set to about 50%. A typical 50-pint unit costs about $38 a month to run 12 hours a day at 18 cents per kWh; see [dehumidifier running cost](/dehumidifier-running-cost) and the [dehumidifier guide](/dehumidifier-guide).

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving seams untaped** or the edges loose, which lets vapor around the barrier.
- **Sealing vents without adding humidity control**, which can trap moisture.
- **Covering standing water or ignoring leaks** instead of fixing drainage first.
- **Using thin plastic where people will crawl**, which tears within a few visits.

## Frequently asked questions

### What thickness vapor barrier should I use in a crawl space?

At least 6-mil polyethylene to block vapor; 10- to 12-mil reinforced if anyone will work in the space; 20-mil for full encapsulation.

### Is crawl space encapsulation worth it?

In a humid climate, or where the crawl space has mold, musty odors or damp insulation, usually yes. In a dry climate with a dry crawl space, a ground cover may be all it needs.

### Should I close my crawl space vents?

Only as part of a full encapsulation with humidity control. Closing vents without a sealed liner and a dehumidifier can make moisture worse.

### Does a crawl space moisture barrier stop mold?

It removes the main moisture source, which stops most mold growth. Keeping humidity below 60%, as the EPA recommends, keeps it that way.
