## What is R-Value and Why It Matters

R-value measures thermal resistance – a material's ability to resist heat flow. Higher R-values indicate better insulating properties and greater energy savings potential.

### Understanding Thermal Resistance

**Heat Transfer Mechanics**: Heat naturally flows from warm to cool areas through three methods:
- **Conduction**: Direct contact heat transfer through materials
- **Convection**: Heat transfer through air movement
- **Radiation**: Heat transfer through electromagnetic waves

**R-Value Definition**: R-value represents thermal resistance per inch of thickness. An R-15 insulation provides 15 times more thermal resistance than one inch of air.

**Temperature Differential Impact**: R-value effectiveness increases with greater temperature differences between indoor and outdoor conditions.

### Economic Impact of Proper R-Values

According to Department of Energy studies, proper insulation R-values provide:

- **Energy Savings**: 15-50% reduction in heating and cooling costs
- **Comfort Improvement**: More consistent indoor temperatures
- **HVAC Efficiency**: Reduced system runtime and wear
- **Home Value**: 3-5% increase in property value
- **Environmental Benefits**: Reduced carbon footprint

### R-Value vs U-Value

**R-Value**: Thermal resistance (higher is better for insulation)
**U-Value**: Thermal transmittance (lower is better, U = 1/R)

Example: R-30 insulation has U-value of 0.033, meaning excellent thermal resistance.

## Insulation Material R-Values Comparison

Different insulation materials provide varying R-values per inch, affecting installation thickness and cost.

**Batt Insulation R-Values**:
- 3.5" thick: R-11 to R-15
- 5.5" thick: R-19 to R-21
- 8.25" thick: R-25 to R-30
- 12" thick: R-38

**Blown-In Fiberglass**:
- R-value per inch: 2.2 to 2.7

- Coverage: 40-60 sq ft per bag

- R-value per inch: 3.2 to 3.8

- Higher R-value per inch than fiberglass

## Calculating R-Value for Your Home

Accurate R-value calculations ensure optimal insulation performance and code compliance.

### Existing Insulation Assessment

**Visual Inspection**:
- Measure current insulation thickness
- Identify insulation type and condition
- Document gaps, compression, or damage
- Check vapor barrier integrity

**R-Value Calculation**:
1. Measure insulation thickness in inches
2. Multiply by material R-value per inch
3. Account for settling (subtract 10-20% for older insulation)
4. Add R-values for multiple layers

**Example Calculation**:
Existing: 6" fiberglass batt (R-19) + 4" blown cellulose (4 × 3.2 = R-12.8)
Total existing R-value: R-19 + R-12.8 = R-31.8

### Required R-Value Calculation

**Determine Climate Zone**: Use ZIP code lookup tools or climate zone maps.

**Code Requirements**: Check local building codes for minimum R-values.

**Energy Star Recommendations**: Often exceed code minimums for optimal performance.

**Additional R-Value Needed**:
Target R-value - Existing R-value = Additional R-value required

### Installation Thickness Calculation

**Example for R-38 Attic in Climate Zone 4**:

Existing R-19 fiberglass + Additional R-19 needed

**Blown Cellulose Addition**:
R-19 ÷ 3.2 R-value per inch = 5.9 inches additional thickness

**Fiberglass Batt Addition**:
Add R-19 batt (5.5" thickness) over existing insulation

### Comprehensive R-Value Assessment

**Thermal Bridging**: Account for framing members reducing overall R-value by 10-25%.

**Air Leakage**: Seal gaps before insulation installation to maintain R-value effectiveness.

**Installation Quality**: Poor installation can reduce R-value by 20-50%.

## Attic Insulation R-Value Requirements

Attic insulation provides the greatest energy savings potential due to stack effect and temperature differentials.

### Attic R-Value by Climate Zone

**Recommended R-Values**:
- Zone 1-2: R-30 to R-49
- Zone 3: R-30 to R-60  
- Zone 4: R-38 to R-60
- Zone 5-8: R-49 to R-60

**Energy Star Recommendations**: R-49 to R-60 for most U.S. climates

### Attic Insulation Installation Methods

**Blown-In Installation**:
- Even coverage over existing insulation
- Fills around obstacles and irregular spaces
- Proper depth markers ensure consistent R-value

**Batt Installation**:
- Layer perpendicular to existing insulation
- Avoid compression maintaining full R-value
- Cut to fit around obstacles

**Combination Approach**:
- Existing batt insulation + blown-in topping
- Achieves target R-value cost-effectively
- Improves thermal performance

### Attic Air Sealing Priority

**Critical Air Sealing Locations**:
- Electrical penetrations
- Plumbing penetrations  
- HVAC duct penetrations
- Recessed lighting fixtures
- Attic access openings

**Air Sealing Materials**:
- Expanding foam for gaps under ¼"
- Weatherstripping for access doors
- Caulk for small cracks and gaps
- Gaskets for electrical boxes

### Ventilation Considerations

**Soffit and Ridge Ventilation**: Maintain airflow with insulation baffles.

**Ventilation Requirements**: 1 sq ft of ventilation per 150 sq ft of attic floor.

**Ice Dam Prevention**: Proper ventilation and insulation prevent ice formation.

## Common R-Value Mistakes

Avoiding installation and selection mistakes ensures rated R-value performance.

### Installation Errors

**Compression**: Compressing insulation reduces R-value proportionally. R-19 compressed to 5.5" performs at R-16.

**Gaps and Voids**: 5% coverage gaps reduce overall R-value by 25-50%.

**Thermal Bridging**: Inadequate attention to framing thermal bridges reduces wall R-value by 15-25%.

**Air Leakage**: Unsealed air leaks bypass insulation reducing effectiveness by 20-40%.

### Material Selection Errors

**Climate Mismatch**: Using minimum code values instead of optimal R-values for climate.

**Moisture Issues**: Incorrect vapor barrier placement causing condensation problems.

**Fire Rating**: Failing to use fire-rated materials in required applications.

### Design Mistakes

**Insufficient Planning**: Not calculating total R-value needs before material purchase.

**Access Issues**: Not considering future attic access when installing insulation.

**Ventilation Blocking**: Installing insulation that blocks required ventilation airflow.

## Energy Savings Calculator

Calculate potential energy savings from insulation R-value upgrades.

### Calculation Methodology

**Heat Loss Formula**:
Heat Loss (BTU/hr) = Area × Temperature Difference ÷ Total R-value

**Example Calculation** (1,200 sq ft attic):

**Before Upgrade** (R-10 existing):
Heat Loss = 1,200 × 50°F ÷ 10 = 6,000 BTU/hr

**After Upgrade** (R-38):
Heat Loss = 1,200 × 50°F ÷ 38 = 1,579 BTU/hr

**Heat Loss Reduction**: 6,000 - 1,579 = 4,421 BTU/hr (74% reduction)

### Annual Savings Estimation

**Heating Degree Days**: Climate-specific calculation factor
**Cooling Degree Days**: Summer cooling load factor
**Energy Costs**: Local electricity and natural gas rates
**HVAC Efficiency**: System efficiency ratings (AFUE, SEER)

**Typical Annual Savings**:
- R-10 to R-30 upgrade: $150-$400 annually
- R-10 to R-49 upgrade: $200-$600 annually
- Complete home insulation: $300-$1,200 annually

### Return on Investment

**Simple Payback**: Initial cost ÷ Annual savings
**Net Present Value**: Accounts for energy cost inflation
**Home Value Increase**: 3-5% property value improvement

## Immediate Impact Tips (0-2 Hours Implementation)

### Tip 1: Optimize Thermostat Settings

**Implementation Time:** 5 minutes  
**Annual Savings:** $150-400  
**Payback:** Immediate

**Optimal temperature settings:**
- **Summer cooling**: 78°F when home, 85°F when away
- **Winter heating**: 68°F when home, 60°F when away
- **Nighttime**: 65-68°F for comfort and efficiency
- **Vacation**: 55°F winter, 85°F summer

**Temperature adjustment savings:**
- **Each degree higher in summer**: 6-8% cooling cost reduction
- **Each degree lower in winter**: 6-10% heating cost reduction
- **8-hour setback**: 10-15% total energy savings
- **Vacation settings**: 15-25% savings during absence

<Callout type="info" title="Smart Thermostat Advantage">
**Smart thermostats automatically optimize settings** and typically save an additional 10-23% beyond manual programming through learning algorithms and advanced scheduling features.
</Callout>

### Tip 2: Replace Air Filters Monthly

**Implementation Time:** 10 minutes  
**Annual Savings:** $100-300  
**Payback:** Immediate

**Filter maintenance impact:**
- **Clean filter**: Maintains optimal airflow and efficiency
- **Dirty filter**: Reduces airflow and forces the system to work harder
- **Completely blocked filter**: Can shut the system down on a high-limit trip
- **System protection**: Prevents costly repairs and extends lifespan

**Filter selection for efficiency:**
- **MERV 6-8**: Best balance of filtration and airflow
- **MERV 9-11**: For allergy concerns, check system compatibility
- **MERV 12+**: Requires high-efficiency system design
- **Avoid cheap fiberglass**: Poor filtration, frequent replacement

### Tip 3: Seal Air Leaks Around Vents and Ducts

**Implementation Time:** 30-60 minutes  
**Annual Savings:** $200-500  
**Payback:** Immediate

**Common air leak locations:**
- **Vent connections**: Where ducts connect to vents
- **Duct joints**: Connections between duct sections
- **Equipment connections**: Where ducts connect to air handler
- **Wall/ceiling penetrations**: Where ducts pass through barriers

**Sealing materials and techniques:**
- **Duct mastic**: Professional-grade sealant for permanent repairs
- **Metal tape**: Better than cloth "duct tape" for durability
- **Caulk**: For small gaps around vent frames
- **Weatherstripping**: For removable panels and access doors

### Tip 4: Clear Outdoor Unit of Debris

**Implementation Time:** 20 minutes  
**Annual Savings:** $75-200  
**Payback:** Immediate

**Outdoor unit maintenance:**
- **Clear 2-foot radius**: Remove vegetation, debris, and obstructions
- **Clean coils gently**: Garden hose spray from inside out
- **Straighten bent fins**: Use fin comb or careful hand straightening
- **Check refrigerant lines**: Ensure proper insulation and clearances

**Impact of dirty outdoor coils:**
- **Light dirt buildup**: 5-10% efficiency loss
- **Moderate contamination**: 15-25% efficiency loss
- **Heavy buildup**: 25-40% efficiency loss
- **Blocked airflow**: Can cause compressor failure

### Tip 5: Use Ceiling Fans to Extend Comfort Range

**Implementation Time:** 30 minutes (if fans installed)  
**Annual Savings:** $100-250  
**Payback:** 2-6 months (if purchasing fans)

**Ceiling fan efficiency strategy:**
- **Summer operation**: Counterclockwise to create cooling breeze
- **Winter operation**: Clockwise on low speed to circulate warm air
- **Temperature adjustment**: Fans allow 3-4°F thermostat adjustment
- **Energy usage**: Fans use 10-120 watts vs 3,000+ watts for AC

**Optimal fan usage:**
- **Room occupancy**: Only run fans in occupied rooms
- **Speed selection**: Higher speeds for greater cooling effect
- **Size matching**: Proper fan size for room dimensions
- **Professional installation**: Ensures safe and efficient operation

# How Many Watts Does a Gas Furnace Use? (Blower Motor Power)

A gas furnace typically uses **300 to 800 watts** of electricity during a heating cycle — far less than the 10,000–24,000 watts an electric furnace consumes. About 80–90% of that electricity goes to the blower motor, with the rest powering the gas valve, inducer motor, control board, and igniter. A variable-speed (ECM) blower uses 75–200 watts on low speed versus 400–700 watts for a single-speed motor — saving $50–$150/year in electricity.

This matters for sizing a backup generator, understanding your electricity bill, and recognizing the hidden electrical operating cost beyond just gas.

## Measuring Your Furnace's Actual Watt Draw

Nameplate wattage on a gas furnace covers the blower motor and controls only — the natural-gas or propane burner obviously doesn't draw electricity. Actual electrical draw during operation varies with blower stage (PSC vs ECM, low vs high stage) and inducer-motor cycles, so a direct measurement gives a more accurate number than any manufacturer estimate.

To measure directly, use a clamp-on ammeter around one of the two hot conductors at the furnace's dedicated 120 V circuit:

1. **Turn off the furnace at the disconnect switch** or breaker before opening any electrical compartment.
2. **Open the wiring compartment** and identify the incoming hot conductor. (If your furnace is 240 V, both hot legs must be considered — but most residential gas furnaces run on a dedicated 120 V circuit.)
3. **Clamp the ammeter around ONE hot conductor** — never both, since the two would cancel to zero.
4. **Restore power.** Wait for the furnace to cycle through startup: inducer motor draws first, then ignition, then main blower comes on after a delay.
5. **Read the amperage** at each stage. Multiply by voltage (120 V for the blower + controls circuit) to get watts.

Typical readings for a mid-size residential gas furnace:

- Standby (electronics only): a few watts
- Inducer motor running: 60–120 W depending on inducer size
- Blower motor on low: PSC blowers roughly 250–400 W; ECM blowers often 100–200 W
- Blower motor on high: PSC 400–600 W; ECM 200–400 W

If the reading is dramatically higher than expected (say 800+ W for a small residential furnace), suspect a bearing issue on the blower or a failing run capacitor causing high current draw. This is a useful early diagnostic — a failing blower motor draws more amperage months before it makes noise or fails outright.

## Battery Backup Sizing for a Gas Furnace

Because the burner runs on gas but the blower runs on electricity, a small battery backup can keep the furnace circulating heat through a grid outage — much cheaper than a whole-house generator sized for total home load.

Sizing math is straightforward:

1. **Furnace peak wattage** — take the highest-stage blower + inducer figure. Add 30% safety margin for startup surge. For a typical 500 W PSC-blower furnace: 500 × 1.3 = 650 W peak draw.
2. **Battery continuous output** — must exceed peak draw. A 1,000 W continuous / 2,000 W peak battery covers a 650 W furnace comfortably.
3. **Battery capacity for runtime** — capacity in Wh ÷ average draw in W = hours. A 1,000 Wh battery at 400 W average blower draw (blower cycles on/off with thermostat calls) delivers 2.5 hours of continuous fan runtime, or roughly 5–8 hours of intermittent operation matching typical thermostat cycling in cold weather.

For multi-day outages, larger battery packs (2,000–5,000 Wh) or a battery + small inverter-generator combo covers the furnace while limiting fuel consumption. Whole-home battery systems (13.5 kWh class) handle a gas furnace easily plus dozens of hours of other essential loads.

The wiring side matters: batteries connected via a simple manual transfer switch feed the furnace only; interlocked automatic transfer switches feed the whole panel. For a furnace-only backup, the manual approach is simpler and cheaper. Consult a licensed electrician for any transfer-switch install — improper wiring can back-feed the utility grid and injure line workers.

## Hidden Costs and Add-Ons

These are the costs that often surprise homeowners when they get the final invoice:

**Ductwork modifications ($500–$3,000+):** If your new furnace is a different size than the old one, or if existing ducts are undersized, damaged, or leaking, ductwork modifications may be necessary. A thorough contractor will inspect your ductwork and flag any issues in the quote.

**Venting conversion ($300–$800):** Upgrading from an 80% AFUE (non-condensing) furnace to a 90%+ AFUE (condensing) furnace requires replacing the metal flue pipe with PVC venting and adding a condensate drain line. This is a standard part of a high-efficiency upgrade but is sometimes quoted as a separate line item.

**Electrical upgrades ($200–$800):** Some new furnaces require a dedicated electrical circuit, especially high-end units with variable-speed blower motors that draw more current. If your existing wiring is undersized, an electrician may be needed.

**Gas line sizing ($200–$1,000):** A larger furnace or one with a higher BTU input may require a larger-diameter gas line. This is more common when upgrading from an older, smaller unit.

**Asbestos abatement ($500–$3,000):** Homes built before 1980 may have asbestos-containing materials in the existing ductwork, insulation, or flue pipe. Removal requires a licensed abatement contractor — this can add significant cost.

**Code compliance upgrades ($200–$1,500):** If your home's HVAC installation doesn't meet current building codes, the contractor or inspector may require upgrades — such as adding a combustion air supply, installing a CO detector hardwired to the system, or upgrading the flue per current standards.

<Callout type="warning">
**Watch for low-ball quotes that exclude these items.** A quote that seems $1,000–$2,000 lower than competitors may not include permits, ductwork modifications, venting changes, or disposal of the old unit. Always ask: "What is NOT included in this price?" and get the answer in writing.
</Callout>

## How to Get the Best Price

**Get at least 3 quotes.** This is the single most effective way to ensure a fair price. Prices for the same furnace and installation can vary by 30–50% between contractors in the same city.

**Time your installation for the off-season.** Late spring (April–May) and early fall (September–October) are the slowest periods for HVAC contractors. Many offer 10–15% discounts or promotional pricing during these months. Avoid emergency replacements in December and January when demand and prices peak.

**Ask about manufacturer rebates.** Carrier, Trane, Lennox, and other brands periodically offer $200–$1,000 rebates on qualifying equipment. These promotions are most common in spring and fall.

**Check for utility rebates.** Many gas and electric utilities offer rebates for high-efficiency furnaces. Typical rebate: $200–$500 for a 95%+ AFUE furnace. Check your utility's website or the DSIRE database (dsireusa.org).

**Consider financing.** Most HVAC companies offer financing through providers like GreenSky or Synchrony with promotional 0% APR for 12–36 months. If you can pay it off within the promotional period, this is essentially free money. Avoid financing that locks you into high interest rates after the promotional period ends.

**Don't automatically choose the cheapest quote.** The lowest bid may come from an unlicensed contractor, skip permits, or cut corners on ductwork and venting. Poor installation is the #1 cause of furnace inefficiency, comfort problems, and premature failure. Verify that every contractor is licensed, insured, and pulls permits.

## What to Expect on Installation Day

A typical furnace replacement takes **4–8 hours** from start to finish for a straightforward swap. Here's the process:

**Before installation:**
- Contractor performs final measurements and confirms equipment selection.
- You clear a path to the furnace area and move any stored items.
- Contractor pulls necessary permits (if not done during the quoting phase).

**During installation (4–8 hours):**
1. Disconnect and remove old furnace (30–60 minutes).
2. Inspect and modify ductwork connections as needed (30–90 minutes).
3. Set new furnace in place and connect ductwork (60–90 minutes).
4. Connect gas line (gas furnace) or electrical (electric furnace) (30–60 minutes).
5. Install or modify venting (30–90 minutes for condensing conversions).
6. Install condensate drain/pump if needed (15–30 minutes).
7. Connect thermostat wiring (15–30 minutes).
8. System startup, combustion analysis, and testing (30–60 minutes).

- The contractor walks you through the new system's operation, thermostat, and filter location.
- They should provide all warranty registration information and paperwork.

**Insist on a combustion analysis at startup.** A proper installation includes measuring gas pressure at the manifold, verifying the temperature rise across the heat exchanger, and performing a combustion analysis to ensure CO levels are within spec. This 15-minute test confirms the furnace is operating safely and efficiently. If your contractor doesn't do this automatically, ask for it.
</Callout>

# Furnace Blowing Cold Air: 7 Causes & Easy Fixes

If your furnace is blowing cold air, the most likely cause is your thermostat fan set to "ON" instead of "AUTO" — which circulates unheated air continuously even when the burner is off. The second most common cause is a dirty air filter triggering a high-limit safety shutdown. Both are free, 30-second fixes that account for roughly 50% of "cold air" complaints.

Here are all 7 causes ranked from easiest to hardest, with step-by-step fixes for each.

### Cause 1: Thermostat Fan Set to "ON" Instead of "AUTO"

**The problem:** When your thermostat's fan setting is "ON," the blower motor runs continuously — 24/7 — regardless of whether the burner is firing. Between heating cycles, the blower pushes unheated air through the ducts. This air is whatever temperature your basement or utility room happens to be (often 55–65°F), which feels cold compared to the 120–140°F supply air during a heating cycle.

**The fix:** Change the fan setting from ON to AUTO. In AUTO mode, the blower only runs when the burner is actively heating. The air coming from your vents will always be warm.

**Why it's confusing:** Some homeowners (or HVAC techs) set the fan to ON thinking it improves air circulation or filtration. While that's technically true, the trade-off is cold air between heating cycles and an extra $15–$30/month in electricity for the blower motor running continuously.

### Cause 2: Clogged Air Filter

**The problem:** A filthy, clogged air filter restricts airflow so severely that the heat exchanger overheats. When the heat exchanger's internal temperature exceeds the safety threshold (usually 160–200°F), the high-limit switch shuts off the burner. But the blower keeps running to cool down the exchanger — blowing unheated air through your vents.

The cycle goes: burner fires → heat exchanger overheats → high-limit trips → burner shuts off → blower runs (blowing cold air) → heat exchanger cools → burner fires again → repeat. This is called short cycling, and it means you get intermittent warm and cold air.

**The fix:** Pull out your air filter and inspect it. If you can't see light through it, or it's visibly packed with dirt, pet hair, and debris, replace it immediately. Keep the furnace running — it may take 10–15 minutes for the high-limit switch to reset after the new filter restores proper airflow.

<Callout type="tip">
**Pro tip:** Keep 2–3 spare filters on hand so you never have to wait for a store trip. A 3-pack of 1-inch pleated MERV 8 filters costs $10–$20 and covers 3–9 months of changes. This is the cheapest insurance against preventable furnace problems.
</Callout>

### Cause 3: Pilot Light or Ignition Failure

**The problem:** If the burner can't ignite, the blower may still activate on a timer — running on schedule but blowing air that was never heated. Modern furnaces with hot surface igniters will attempt ignition 3–4 times before locking out. During those failed attempts, you may feel the blower running with no heat.

**Older furnaces with standing pilot lights:** If the pilot light has gone out, the main burner can't ignite. Relight it following the instructions printed on the furnace (usually: turn gas valve to PILOT, hold down the pilot button, use a long lighter to ignite the pilot, hold for 30 seconds, then release and turn to ON).

**Modern furnaces with electronic ignition:** If the hot surface igniter (HSI) has failed (cracked or burned out), the burner won't light. You'll typically see the furnace start its sequence — inducer motor runs, then nothing happens. A blinking error light on the control board will indicate "ignition failure."

**The fix for electronic ignition:** This usually requires professional repair. An igniter replacement costs $150–$350 including parts and labor. The igniter itself is a $15–$50 part.

### Cause 4: Dirty Flame Sensor

**The problem:** The flame sensor is a metal rod that sits in the burner flame and confirms ignition. If it's dirty (coated with carbon, oxidation, or soot), it can't detect the flame. The result: the burner lights for 3–10 seconds, the flame sensor fails to confirm it, and the control board shuts off the gas valve. The blower continues running — blowing cold air.

This is the #1 service call for HVAC technicians during the heating season.

**The fix:** Turn off the furnace and gas. Locate the flame sensor (a thin metal rod near the burner, opposite the igniter). Remove the single mounting screw, pull it out, and gently clean the rod with fine-grit emery cloth or sandpaper. Reinstall it, restore power and gas, and test. Total time: 10–15 minutes.

### Cause 5: Overheating (High-Limit Switch Tripping)

**The problem:** The high-limit switch is a safety device that shuts off the burner if the heat exchanger gets too hot. While a clogged filter is the most common trigger (Cause 2), other causes include:

- Blocked or closed supply vents (furniture or rugs covering vents)
- Undersized ductwork or too many closed registers
- Failed blower motor capacitor (blower runs slowly)
- Blower motor running at too low a speed setting

**The fix:** Open all supply and return vents, clear any obstructions, and check the filter. If the problem persists with a clean filter and open vents, the blower motor or its capacitor may need professional attention ($150–$500).

### Cause 6: Gas Supply Issue

**The problem:** If the gas supply to the furnace is interrupted, the burner can't fire. The blower may still activate on a timer, circulating unheated air. Check for:

- Gas valve near the furnace turned off (handle perpendicular to pipe = closed)
- Gas meter shut-off turned off
- Gas utility outage (check if other gas appliances work — stove, water heater)
- Empty propane tank (if applicable)

**The fix:** Verify the gas valve is open (handle parallel to pipe). If other gas appliances aren't working either, call your gas utility — there may be a service outage. If only the furnace is affected, the furnace gas valve may have failed (professional repair needed).

### Cause 7: Ductwork Leak or Disconnection

**The problem:** A disconnected duct section, large hole, or severe leak in the ductwork between the furnace and the rooms can cause what feels like cold air at the registers. Heated air escapes into the attic, basement, or crawl space before reaching the rooms. What makes it to the registers has cooled significantly.

Signs of ductwork issues include: certain rooms that are always cold, visible gaps in ductwork at joints, higher-than-expected energy bills, and dusty conditions in the home.

**The fix:** Inspect accessible ductwork for visible disconnections or damage. Duct sealing (with mastic or metal tape — NOT fabric "duct tape") can fix small leaks. Large disconnections or ductwork in inaccessible areas require professional repair ($200–$1,000+).

- **Check the thermostat fan setting first.** Switching from ON to AUTO is the single most common fix for "cold air" complaints — it takes 5 seconds and costs nothing.
- **A clogged filter** forces the furnace to overheat and shut off the burner, leaving the blower running with unheated air. Replace it immediately if it's dirty.
- **A dirty flame sensor** causes the burner to ignite briefly then shut off. This is a 10-minute DIY fix with sandpaper.
