## Garage BTU Sizing Guide

Garages have unique sizing challenges: they're often poorly insulated, have large door openings, contain heat-generating equipment, and experience massive temperature swings. Standard residential BTU calculations undersize for garages.

| Garage Type | Size (sq ft) | Insulated | Recommended BTU | Recommended Unit |
|-------------|-------------|-----------|-----------------|-----------------|
| 1-car | 200–300 | No | 12,000–15,000 | 12K or 15K |
| 1-car | 200–300 | Yes | 9,000–12,000 | 9K or 12K |
| 2-car | 400–500 | No | 18,000–24,000 | 18K or 24K |
| 2-car | 400–500 | Yes | 12,000–18,000 | 12K or 18K |
| 3-car | 600–800 | No | 24,000–36,000 | 24K or 36K |
| 3-car | 600–800 | Yes | 18,000–24,000 | 18K or 24K |

### Worked Example: Uninsulated 2-Car Garage in Dallas, TX

- Garage: 450 sq ft, uninsulated walls, single-pane windows, 10-ft ceilings
- Base: 450 × 30 = 13,500 BTU
- Uninsulated: +30% → +4,050 BTU
- High ceilings: +25% → +3,375 BTU
- Hot climate: +15% → +2,025 BTU
- Adjusted: 22,950 BTU
- **Recommended: 24,000 BTU unit**

### Worked Example: Insulated 1-Car Garage in Portland, OR

- Garage: 250 sq ft, insulated walls, 8-ft ceilings
- Base: 250 × 30 = 7,500 BTU
- Good insulation: no adjustment
- Mild climate: no adjustment
- Adjusted: 7,500 BTU
- **Recommended: 9,000 BTU unit**

## Insulation-Tier BTU Adjustment

The sizing table above assumes a moderately insulated envelope. Real garages sit on a wide spectrum, and the sensible load can move 30–50 percent between adjacent tiers before ceiling height and climate multipliers even enter the calculation.

**Bare-shell garage (no wall insulation, uninsulated overhead door, unsealed door bottom, slab-on-grade).** Typical practice is to add 30–50 percent to the base BTU-per-square-foot figure before layering in the other multipliers. A 400 sq ft garage that would need 12,000 BTU at moderate insulation can climb to 18,000–20,000 BTU once wall and door losses are counted, which is why the uninsulated rows in the sizing table jump a full BTU tier rather than a fractional step.

**Weatherized garage (R-13 batts in the walls, insulated overhead door, perimeter weatherstripping, air-sealed man door).** These upgrades commonly drop the load enough to move down one BTU tier — from 24K to 18K on a 2-car, or from 18K to 12K on a 1-car. Department of Energy weatherization guidance identifies the overhead door and its perimeter as the largest single loss on a typical garage face, so a foam-panel kit for the door and a fresh bottom seal are usually the highest-return upgrades before equipment is sized.

**Detached versus attached.** A detached garage loses heat through all six surfaces (four walls, roof, and slab) while an attached garage borrows one wall from conditioned space and sees less infiltration at the shared boundary. On the same square footage and insulation level, a detached garage typically justifies sizing one step higher — closer to the uninsulated row than the insulated row — until the shell is genuinely tightened.

The practical order of operations is: weatherstrip and door-insulate first, size the mini split second. A tighter envelope also lets the inverter compressor cycle at lower speeds where it is most efficient, which widens the gap between the nameplate SEER2 and what the unit delivers over a season.

## Electrical for Garage Installs

Electrical readiness is the most commonly underestimated line item in a garage mini split project. The FAQ below covers the NEC 210.23 and NEC 440 dedicated-circuit rule for fixed HVAC and the 120V-versus-240V equipment choice; this section focuses on the panel-side scope that determines whether the wiring work is trivial or a small project of its own.

**Subpanel capacity is the real bottleneck.** Detached garages commonly run from a 60A, 100A, or 150A subpanel fed by a single feeder from the main house. Those panels are often already loaded with lighting, receptacles, an EV charger, and shop equipment. A full panel forces one of three moves: swap two full-size breakers for a tandem to free a double-pole slot (only where the panel is listed for tandems in that position), upgrade the subpanel to a higher slot count, or run a larger feeder from the main.

**Feeder ampacity is easy to miss.** Even when the subpanel has physical slot room, the feeder from the main panel may not have headroom for the added load once existing continuous loads are counted under NEC load-calc rules. A licensed electrician runs the calculation before adding the breaker; if the feeder is undersized the fix is a heavier conductor run all the way back to the main panel — often the most expensive part of the electrical scope.

**Verify voltage from the AHRI cert before ordering.** Spec sheets sometimes list multiple voltage variants under one model family — a 12K model, for example, may exist in both a 115V and a 208/230V version, with different MCA and MOCP ratings. The AHRI Directory certification listing pins the exact SKU that matches a given voltage and overcurrent protection, so matching the equipment order to the planned circuit before purchase avoids an expensive reversal after the electrician quotes the run.
