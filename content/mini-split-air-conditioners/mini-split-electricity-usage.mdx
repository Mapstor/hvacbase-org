# How Much Electricity Does a Mini Split Use?

You want to know what a mini split actually costs to run before you commit, or you've got one and your electric bill went up and you're wondering if it's the culprit. Either way, the honest answer is that mini splits are among the most efficient ways to heat and cool, but the actual number depends on your unit's size, your climate, how you use it, and your local electricity rate.

This guide breaks down how much electricity a mini split really uses, in watts, kWh, and dollars, plus the factors that push the number up or down and the practical steps that lower it. The wattage figures are typical ranges (your unit's rated draw is on its nameplate), and cost figures use current electricity-rate data. Use the calculator to get a number for your specific situation.

**A typical 12,000 BTU mini split draws roughly 400 to 1,200 watts depending on how hard the compressor is working, which comes to about 3 to 10 kWh per day at 8 hours of use, or roughly 500 to 900 kWh over a cooling season.** The key thing that makes mini splits efficient: their inverter compressors modulate speed, so once a room reaches temperature the unit throttles way down and draws far less than its maximum rating. Your actual cost is that energy use multiplied by your local electricity rate, use the calculator below for your exact figure.

## Rated watts vs. what it actually draws

Every mini split has a *rated* wattage, its maximum draw at full compressor speed. But here's what matters for your bill: inverter mini splits rarely run at maximum. They typically operate at **30 to 70% of capacity** once a room reaches the set temperature, throttling down to just maintain it. So the "average" draw over a day is well below the rated number.

| Mini split size | Rated (max) watts | Typical average watts (cooling) |
|-----------------|-------------------|--------------------------------|
| 6,000 BTU | ~450–650W | ~200–400W |
| 9,000 BTU | ~600–800W | ~300–500W |
| 12,000 BTU | ~800–1,200W | ~400–700W |
| 18,000 BTU | ~1,200–1,800W | ~600–1,100W |
| 24,000 BTU | ~1,600–2,400W | ~900–1,500W |
| 36,000 BTU | ~2,400–3,600W | ~1,400–2,200W |

**Heating mode** typically draws somewhat more than cooling for the same BTU output, because the outdoor unit works harder to extract heat from cold air, and in extreme cold, supplemental electric resistance heat may engage, which raises consumption significantly. That's why a mini split's heating cost depends heavily on your climate.

## What drives your usage up or down

Your actual electricity use can swing widely from the typical figures based on these factors:

| Factor | Effect on usage | Why |
|--------|-----------------|-----|
| Bigger indoor/outdoor temperature gap | Higher | Unit works harder to bridge a larger difference |
| Poor insulation | Higher | Room gains or loses heat faster |
| Dirty filters | Higher | Restricted airflow forces the compressor to work harder |
| Dirty coils | Higher | Reduced heat-transfer efficiency |
| Wrong-sized unit (over or under) | Higher | Oversized short-cycles; undersized runs at max continuously |
| Sunny, unshaded room | Higher | Solar heat gain adds to the cooling load |
| More occupants and heat-generating equipment | Higher | People and appliances add heat the unit must remove |
| Lower SEER2 rating | Higher | Less efficient conversion of electricity to cooling |

The pattern: a right-sized unit, in a well-insulated room, with clean filters, run at a sensible temperature, uses far less than a neglected or poorly-matched one.

## How to cut your mini split's electricity use

These practical steps genuinely reduce consumption:

**Set the temperature sensibly.** Per the Department of Energy, you save on energy costs by setting the thermostat closer to the outdoor temperature (roughly on the order of a percent or so of energy use per degree over an extended period). In cooling, every degree warmer you can tolerate reduces the load; the DOE notes that setting it colder than you need doesn't cool any faster, it just runs up the bill.

**Clean the filters regularly.** A clogged filter is one of the biggest efficiency killers, per the DOE it can increase energy use by 5 to 15% by restricting airflow. Most mini split filters are washable, check them roughly monthly and rinse when dirty.

**Use dry mode in humid conditions.** In humid (but not very hot) weather, dry mode removes moisture while the compressor runs at low speed, so it generally uses less power than full cooling. Exact savings vary by unit (it's a manufacturer feature without a fixed standard), see our [dry mode guide](/dry-mode-in-ac) for how it works.

**Shade the outdoor unit.** A condenser in direct sun works a bit harder than one in shade, so shading it (while keeping the manufacturer's required airflow clearance, and never enclosing it) helps efficiency.

**Improve the room's insulation.** Weatherstripping, insulating window film, or thermal curtains reduce the heat your room gains or loses, so the mini split works less. These are low-cost improvements that pay back over time.

**Keep up with maintenance.** Clean coils and an annual check keep the system running at its rated efficiency; a neglected unit drifts toward the higher end of the usage ranges.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much electricity does a mini split use per month?**
It depends on the unit size, how many hours it runs, your climate, and your rate. A 12,000 BTU unit running several hours a day might use roughly 150 to 300 kWh a month in active cooling season, more in extreme heat or cold. Multiply your kWh by your electricity rate for the cost, or use the calculator for an estimate.

**Are mini splits cheaper to run than central air?**
Generally yes. Mini splits avoid the 20 to 30% duct losses the DOE attributes to typical duct systems, and their inverter compressors modulate to match demand, so they usually use less electricity than a comparable ducted system. See our [mini split vs. central air](/mini-split-vs-central-air) comparison.

**Does a mini split use a lot of electricity in winter?**
It can, in cold climates. Heating draws more than cooling for the same output, and in extreme cold, supplemental electric resistance heat may engage, raising consumption. A cold-climate-rated model is more efficient at low temperatures. Your heating cost depends heavily on your climate and electricity rate.

**How can I reduce my mini split's electricity use?**
Set the temperature sensibly (closer to outdoor temp), clean the filters regularly (a dirty filter can add 5 to 15% per the DOE), use dry mode in humid weather, shade the outdoor unit, and improve room insulation. A right-sized, well-maintained unit run at a reasonable temperature uses far less than a neglected one.

**What uses more electricity, a mini split or window AC?**
For the same cooling, a mini split's inverter compressor is typically more efficient than a standard window unit's single-speed compressor, especially over a full season, because it modulates rather than cycling fully on and off.
