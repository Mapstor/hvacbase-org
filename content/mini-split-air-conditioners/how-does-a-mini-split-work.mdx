# What Is a Mini Split, and How Does It Work?

You've got a mini split, or you're about to, and you want to actually understand what's happening inside it, not just "it heats and cools." Maybe you're trying to figure out why it's so efficient, or whether it'll really keep up in winter, or you just like knowing how the thing on your wall works. Either way, the mechanism is genuinely clever once you see it.

This is a plain-English explanation of what a mini split is and how it works, from the physics of moving heat to why the inverter compressor saves energy, plus when one is the right choice. Efficiency figures come from ENERGY STAR and the Department of Energy.

**A mini split works by moving heat instead of creating it, using refrigerant to carry heat between an outdoor unit and one or more indoor units.** In cooling mode it pulls heat out of your room and dumps it outside; in heating mode it runs in reverse, pulling heat from the outdoor air (even cold air) and releasing it inside. A variable-speed compressor adjusts its output to match demand precisely, which is a big part of why it's so efficient.

## The key idea: it moves heat, it doesn't make it

This is the concept that makes everything else click. A furnace *creates* heat by burning fuel. An electric baseboard heater *creates* heat by forcing electricity through resistance.

A mini split does neither. It's a **heat pump**, and it simply *moves* heat from one place to another, the same way your refrigerator moves heat out of the fridge compartment to keep your food cold.

Why does that matter? Because moving heat takes far less energy than creating it. That's the whole reason a mini split can deliver, per ENERGY STAR, up to about **three times more heat energy than the electricity it consumes**.

A resistance heater is capped at 100% efficient (one unit of electricity makes one unit of heat). A heat pump effectively beats that ceiling by not making heat at all, it relocates heat that already exists in the air. Against standard electric radiators like baseboard heaters, ENERGY STAR says a certified mini split can use up to 60% less energy.

And yes, **cold air still contains heat**. "Cold" is relative, even air at 20°F holds a lot of thermal energy compared to, say, 0°F. A heat pump can extract that heat and concentrate it, which is why modern cold-climate mini splits keep working well below freezing.

## The refrigeration cycle: how the heat actually moves

The heat-moving trick relies on a special fluid called refrigerant, and four key components working in a loop. Here's the cycle in cooling mode:

<RefrigerationCycle caption="The vapor-compression cycle a mini split uses. The same four components run in reverse for heating, moving heat from outside to inside instead." />

1. **The evaporator (indoor unit).** Warm room air blows across a cold coil filled with low-pressure refrigerant. The refrigerant absorbs heat from the air (which is why the air coming out feels cool) and boils into a gas. The room loses heat; the refrigerant gains it.

2. **The compressor (outdoor unit).** This gas travels outside to the compressor, which squeezes it, raising its pressure and temperature. Now the refrigerant is hotter than the outdoor air.

3. **The condenser (outdoor unit).** The hot, high-pressure refrigerant flows through the outdoor coil, and because it's now hotter than the outside air, it releases its heat to the outdoors and condenses back into a liquid. This is the heat from your room being dumped outside.

4. **The expansion valve.** The liquid refrigerant passes through a valve that drops its pressure sharply, which makes it very cold again. It returns to the indoor coil to absorb more heat, and the cycle repeats.

**For heating, the whole cycle reverses** (a component called a reversing valve flips the flow). Now the outdoor coil absorbs heat from the outside air and the indoor coil releases it into your room. Same four parts, same refrigerant, opposite direction.

## Why "ductless" makes it efficient

A mini split delivers this conditioned air directly from the indoor head mounted in your room, with no ductwork in between. That matters more than it sounds. The DOE puts duct air losses at about 30% of a cooling system's energy consumption, as air leaks out of duct seams and heat bleeds out as the air travels through unconditioned attics and crawlspaces.

By skipping ducts entirely, a mini split loses none of that. The refrigerant carries the heat right up to the coil in your room, and the conditioned air comes straight out of the head.

Nothing is lost in transit. This **"zero duct loss"** is one of the two main reasons mini splits outperform central systems on efficiency.

## The inverter compressor: the other efficiency secret

The second reason is how the compressor runs. Older central systems (and cheap window units) use a single-speed compressor that's either **full-blast or off**. It cools hard until the room hits the target, shuts off, then kicks back on when the room warms up, an energy-hungry on-off cycle.

A mini split uses an **inverter, or variable-speed, compressor.** Instead of switching fully on and off, it continuously adjusts its speed to match the heating or cooling the room needs, then ramps down and runs gently to *maintain* the temperature.

This saves energy by avoiding wasteful hard restarts, and keeps the temperature steadier and the unit quieter, without big swings between too cold and too warm.

Together, the inverter compressor and zero duct loss are why, per the DOE, mini splits reach SEER2 efficiency ratings **up to about 35**, well beyond what most ducted systems achieve.

## How multiple rooms work (zoning)

In a multi-zone mini split, one outdoor unit connects to several indoor heads, one per room or area, through separate refrigerant lines.

Each head has its own controls and can **run independently**. That's why you can cool the bedroom while leaving the living room alone, or heat only the rooms you're using. The outdoor unit's inverter compressor adjusts its total output to serve whichever heads are calling for heating or cooling at that moment.

This independent, room-by-room control is a direct result of the ductless design, there's no shared duct system forcing the whole house to one setting.

Heads come in several styles to suit different rooms: wall-mounted, ceiling-cassette, and even concealed ducted units that feed a short run of duct to one area.

## When a mini split makes sense (and when it doesn't)

**A mini split is often a great fit if:**
- **Your home has no ductwork** (older homes with radiators or baseboard heat); retrofitting ducts is expensive and disruptive, and a mini split avoids it.
- **You have costly electric heat** (baseboard, electric furnace, wall heaters), where the efficiency gain over resistance heating is large.
- **You have a problem room**, an addition, converted garage, sunroom, or a space that's always too hot or cold that your central system can't handle well.
- **You want zoned control** and are tired of hot and cold spots.

**Central air might be the better choice if:**
- **Your home already has good ductwork** in decent condition, where a high-efficiency ducted system may make more sense.
- **You're conditioning a very large open space** where multiple heads would be impractical.

The number and size of heads depends on each room's load; our [mini-split sizing calculator](/mini-split-sizing-calculator) helps you estimate it, and [mini split vs. central air](/mini-split-vs-central-air) weighs the two side by side.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a mini split system?**
A ductless heat pump that heats and cools without ductwork, with one or more indoor units (heads) on walls or ceilings connected by a thin refrigerant line to an outdoor unit. Because it skips ducts and uses variable-speed technology, it is typically more efficient than central systems and allows room-by-room control.

**How is a mini split different from regular AC?**
The main difference is no ductwork, and a mini split also heats, since it is a heat pump, not just an air conditioner. Central AC pushes air through ducts that lose energy, while a mini split delivers conditioned air directly from a head in each room and offers per-room zoning.

**Can a mini split heat and cool a whole house?**
Yes. With enough indoor heads, a multi-zone system can condition a whole home, though it is also commonly used to supplement an existing system or handle specific rooms. The right number and size of heads depends on each room's load.

**How does a mini split work in simple terms?**
It moves heat rather than creating it. Refrigerant absorbs heat from the air in one place and releases it in another, an indoor unit and an outdoor unit connected by a refrigerant line.

In summer it moves heat out of your home; in winter it reverses to move heat from outside air into your home. A variable-speed compressor matches its output to demand.

**How does a mini split heat when it's cold outside?**
Even cold air contains thermal energy, and a heat pump can extract it. In heating mode, the outdoor coil absorbs heat from the outside air, the compressor concentrates it, and the indoor coil releases it into your room. Modern cold-climate models are designed to do this efficiently well below freezing.

**Why are mini splits more efficient than central AC?**
Two reasons. First, no ducts, so they avoid the duct losses the DOE puts at about 30% of a cooling system's energy consumption. Second, the inverter compressor varies its speed to match demand instead of cycling fully on and off, which wastes less energy and holds a steadier temperature.

**What does the refrigerant do in a mini split?**
Refrigerant is the fluid that carries heat. It absorbs heat and boils into a gas at the coil where heat is being removed, then releases that heat and condenses back to liquid at the other coil. Cycling between these states, driven by the compressor and expansion valve, is how the system moves heat from one place to another.

**What is an inverter compressor?**
A variable-speed compressor that continuously adjusts its output to match the heating or cooling demand, rather than running full-blast or off like a single-speed compressor. It ramps down to gently maintain temperature once the room is comfortable, which saves energy and keeps the temperature and noise steadier.

## About these figures

The efficiency figures are from primary sources: the DOE puts duct air losses at about 30% of a cooling system's energy consumption, the "up to 3x more heat energy than electricity used" heating efficiency is from **ENERGY STAR**, and the SEER2 rating range (up to about 35) is from the **U.S. Department of Energy.** The description of the vapor-compression refrigeration cycle (evaporator, compressor, condenser, expansion valve) reflects the standard operating principle of all heat-pump and air-conditioning systems. The "up to 60% less energy than standard electric radiators" figure is from ENERGY STAR's ductless heating and cooling resources.
