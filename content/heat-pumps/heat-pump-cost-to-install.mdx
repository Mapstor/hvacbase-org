# Heat Pump Installation Cost: Complete Pricing Guide (2026)

You're considering a heat pump and the first thing you need is a realistic budget, not a lowball number that balloons once the quotes come in. Heat pump installation is a significant investment, and the price swings a lot based on the type of system, your home, and where you live.

This guide gives you honest cost ranges by system type and size, breaks down where the money actually goes (equipment vs. labor), covers the factors that push a quote higher, and lays out the current 2026 tax credits and rebates, which changed recently. The figures are typical ranges from contractor pricing, not fixed quotes, so always get several itemized bids for your actual home. We don't recommend specific brands, since install quality matters more than the badge on the unit.

**A ducted air-source heat pump typically costs roughly $5,800 to $10,000 installed for a 3-ton system in 2026; a single-zone mini split runs about $2,700 to $5,800, and a ground-source (geothermal) system is much more, roughly $18,000 to $35,000 before incentives.** Your actual cost depends on system type, size, efficiency, your home's electrical and ductwork situation, and your region (California and the Northeast run higher). Get at least three itemized quotes.

## Cost by system type

The type of heat pump is the biggest single cost factor:

| System type | Typical installed cost (2026) |
|-------------|-------------------------------|
| Ducted air-source (standard efficiency) | ~$5,800–$10,000 |
| Ducted air-source (high efficiency, variable-speed) | ~$7,500–$12,500 |
| Single-zone mini split | ~$2,700–$5,800 |
| Multi-zone mini split (2–4 zones) | ~$6,500–$19,000 |
| Ground-source / geothermal | ~$18,000–$35,000 |

**Ducted air-source** is the most common, replacing your furnace and AC while using existing ductwork. Higher-efficiency variable-speed units cost more but run quieter and cheaper. **Mini splits** avoid ductwork and add zoning; cost scales with the number of indoor heads. **Geothermal** has by far the highest upfront cost (the ground loop is the expensive part) but the lowest operating cost and longest lifespan, it makes the most sense in new construction or where long-term efficiency justifies the investment. For the ductless option in depth, see our [mini split cost guide](/mini-split-installation-cost).

## Cost by size

Heat pumps are sized in tons (1 ton = 12,000 BTU), and cost scales with capacity. Rough installed ranges for a mid-efficiency ducted system:

| Size | Typical home | Approx. installed cost |
|------|--------------|------------------------|
| 1.5 ton (18,000 BTU) | 600–1,000 sq ft | ~$4,000–$7,000 |
| 2 ton (24,000 BTU) | 1,000–1,400 sq ft | ~$4,700–$8,200 |
| 2.5 ton (30,000 BTU) | 1,400–1,800 sq ft | ~$5,300–$9,000 |
| 3 ton (36,000 BTU) | 1,800–2,200 sq ft | ~$5,800–$10,000 |
| 4 ton (48,000 BTU) | 2,700–3,200 sq ft | ~$7,000–$11,500 |
| 5 ton (60,000 BTU) | 3,200–4,000 sq ft | ~$8,300–$13,000 |

Important: don't size by square footage alone. Proper sizing comes from a Manual J load calculation (per ACCA) that accounts for your insulation, windows, climate, and layout, an oversized heat pump costs more and runs worse. Use our [heat pump size calculator](/heat-pump-size-calculator) for an estimate, and insist your contractor does a real load calculation.

## Where the money goes: equipment vs. labor

Installation labor typically runs 40 to 55% of the total. Understanding the components helps you read a quote:

| Labor component | Typical range | What it covers |
|-----------------|---------------|----------------|
| Remove old equipment | ~$200–$500 | Includes refrigerant recovery |
| Set outdoor unit | ~$300–$600 | Pad, mounting, vibration isolation |
| Install air handler / coil | ~$500–$1,200 | Includes refrigerant piping |
| Refrigerant line set | ~$300–$800 | Length-dependent |
| Electrical connection | ~$300–$800 | Disconnect, whip, wiring |
| Thermostat + wiring | ~$100–$300 | Smart thermostat may add cost |
| System commissioning | ~$200–$400 | Charge, test, verify performance |
| Permits + inspection | ~$100–$500 | Required by most municipalities |

The commissioning line matters more than it looks: a heat pump that isn't properly charged and verified at install underperforms for its whole life, so a contractor who does thorough commissioning is worth paying for.

## What pushes the cost up

Several common factors can push a quote well above the base price, ask about these up front so your bid reflects reality:

- **Electrical panel upgrade.** Homes with a 100-amp panel often need a 200-amp upgrade to handle the heat pump's circuit, roughly $1,500 to $4,000. This is one of the most common surprise costs.
- **New or modified ductwork.** If you lack ducts or the existing ones are in poor shape, new ductwork runs roughly $2,000 to $5,000 for a single story, more for multi-story.
- **Difficult access.** Second-story, tight crawlspace, or attic placements add labor time and cost.
- **Long refrigerant runs** through finished walls, and **cold-climate-rated equipment** (built for very low temperatures), both add to the price.

## 2026 tax credits and rebates (what changed)

This is where the rules shifted recently, so here's the accurate current picture:

**The federal Section 25C tax credit expired for installs placed in service after December 31, 2025.** The 25C credit had covered 30% of project cost up to $2,000 for qualifying high-efficiency heat pumps, but it was terminated under the OBBBA (PL 119-21, signed July 4, 2025). It does not offset 2026 installations. **The Section 25D credit for geothermal also expired** for installs after December 31, 2025.

**What's still active in 2026:**
- **IRA HEAR rebates** (income-qualified): up to $8,000 point-of-sale for heat pumps, income-tiered, state-administered.
- **IRA HOMES rebates:** open to all incomes, performance-based on energy savings, state-administered.
- **State and utility rebates:** many offer substantial incentives, some quite large. Check the DSIRE database (dsireusa.org) or your state energy office for what's currently funded.

Because the state programs roll out on their own timelines and funding levels, confirm what your state offers, and which equipment qualifies, with your installer before signing. See our [heat pump tax credits 2026 guide](/heat-pump-tax-credits-2026) for details.

## How to get the best price

A few practical steps genuinely lower your cost:

- **Get at least three itemized quotes**, and compare them apples-to-apples (same efficiency tier, same included work). Quotes vary widely because scope and equipment differ, not just price.
- **Time it for the shoulder season** (spring or fall). Contractors are less slammed than during the first heat wave or cold snap, and you have more negotiating room.
- **Stack every incentive** you qualify for (state, utility, IRA rebates).
- **Prioritize the contractor over the brand.** Install quality affects performance and lifespan more than which major brand you choose, a well-installed mid-tier system outperforms a premium unit installed poorly. Look for proper licensing, EPA 608 certification, and a willingness to do a Manual J load calculation. Find qualified contractors through ACCA.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it cheaper to install a heat pump or a furnace and AC?**
A gas furnace plus AC typically runs about $4,500 to $6,500 installed, versus roughly $5,800 to $10,000 for a ducted heat pump, so the heat pump usually costs more upfront. The federal 25C credit that used to help close that gap expired for installs after December 31, 2025, though state and utility rebates plus IRA HEAR/HOMES often still narrow it. A heat pump also replaces both heating and cooling in one system and is typically cheaper to run than electric, oil, or propane heating, so it can be the better long-term value depending on your local energy prices.

**How long does heat pump installation take?**
A standard ducted heat pump replacement takes 1 to 2 days; a single-zone mini split 4 to 8 hours; a multi-zone mini split 2 to 3 days; and geothermal 1 to 2 weeks (mostly the ground loop). Add time if an electrical panel upgrade is needed, and note that permit acquisition can add days to weeks before work starts.

**Can I finance a heat pump installation?**
Yes. Common options include utility-sponsored financing (often 0%), PACE loans repaid through property taxes, manufacturer/dealer financing, home equity loans or lines of credit, and personal loans. Compare carefully, some promotional dealer financing jumps to a high rate after the intro period.

**How much does it cost to add a heat pump to an existing furnace?**
Adding a heat pump alongside your gas furnace (a dual-fuel or hybrid system) typically costs roughly $3,500 to $7,000 for the heat pump equipment and installation. You keep the furnace as backup for the coldest days while the heat pump handles moderate-weather heating and all cooling, a popular cold-climate approach that avoids replacing a working furnace.

**Why are heat pump quotes so different from each other?**
Because the scope and equipment differ. Several things move the number:

- Different **efficiency tiers**
- Different **warranty terms**
- Whether **ductwork and electrical work** are included
- **Contractor overhead**
- **Factory-authorized status**

Always compare on an apples-to-apples basis, specify the same efficiency tier and the same included work across bids.
