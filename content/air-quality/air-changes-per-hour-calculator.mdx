# Air Changes per Hour (ACH) Calculator

Air changes per hour is how many times a room's full volume of air is moved, replaced or filtered in an hour. It's a way of comparing airflow across rooms of different sizes, whether the air comes from outdoors, an exhaust fan or an air purifier.

**ACH = airflow in cubic feet per minute × 60 ÷ room volume in cubic feet. A 12 × 15 foot room with an 8-foot ceiling holds 1,440 cubic feet, so 120 CFM moves its air 5 times an hour. Ventilation ACH (fresh outdoor air) and an air purifier's ACH (filtered room air) measure different things.**

## The formula

- **ACH = CFM × 60 ÷ volume.**
- **CFM needed = volume × target ACH ÷ 60.**

Volume is length × width × ceiling height: a 12 × 15 ft room with an 8-foot ceiling holds 1,440 cubic feet. The 60 converts cubic feet per minute to cubic feet per hour.

## From a target back to CFM

The calculator also runs the formula in reverse: set a target and it returns the airflow. Rearranged, CFM needed = volume × target ACH ÷ 60.

Take a 200 sq ft room with a 9-foot ceiling, so a volume of 1,800 cubic feet. To move its air 6 times an hour, you need 1,800 × 6 ÷ 60 = 180 CFM. Halving the target to 3 ACH halves the airflow to 90 CFM, because the two are directly proportional.

## Ventilation vs. filtration

- **Ventilation** brings in outdoor air and exhausts indoor air. ASHRAE Standard 62.2 sets a home's continuous ventilation in cfm: 0.03 per square foot plus 7.5 per person, counted as bedrooms plus one. For a 2,000 sq ft, three-bedroom home that's 90 cfm, which in a 16,000-cubic-foot house is about 0.34 ACH. See [whole-house ventilation](/whole-house-ventilation-systems).
- **Filtration** recirculates room air through a filter. AHAM's two-thirds rule for air purifiers, a smoke CADR of two-thirds of the floor area, works out to about 5 ACH of filtered air at an 8-foot ceiling. See the [air purifier sizing guide](/air-purifier-sizing-guide).

The calculator's per-room targets are common rules of thumb for comparing rooms, not code requirements.

## Turning a purifier's CADR into ACH

A purifier's clean air delivery rate (CADR) is just an airflow in CFM, so it drops straight into the formula: filtered ACH = CADR × 60 ÷ room volume. The table below works that out for three CADR values at an 8-foot ceiling. See the [air purifier sizing guide](/air-purifier-sizing-guide) for matching CADR to a room.

| Room (8-ft ceiling) | CADR 100 | CADR 200 | CADR 300 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 150 sq ft (1,200 cf) | 5.0 ACH | 10.0 ACH | 15.0 ACH |
| 300 sq ft (2,400 cf) | 2.5 ACH | 5.0 ACH | 7.5 ACH |
| 500 sq ft (4,000 cf) | 1.5 ACH | 3.0 ACH | 4.5 ACH |

AHAM's two-thirds rule lands on about 5 ACH: a 300 sq ft room wants a smoke CADR near 200, and 200 × 60 ÷ 2,400 is 5.0. Doubling the CADR doubles the air changes, and doubling the room roughly halves them.

## How fast a given ACH clears the air

In a well-mixed room where the incoming air is clean and nothing new is being added, particle concentration falls exponentially. The fraction still in the air after a given time is e raised to the power of minus ACH times the hours elapsed. So one air change does not empty the room; it clears about 63% of what was there, because e to the minus 1 is 0.37.

The table shows the percent removed at 2, 5 and 8 ACH. At 5 ACH, for instance, 30 minutes is half an hour, so the exponent is minus 2.5, about 8% remains, and 92% is gone.

| Air changes per hour | 15 min | 30 min | 60 min |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 ACH | 39% | 63% | 86% |
| 5 ACH | 71% | 92% | 99% |
| 8 ACH | 86% | 98% | ~100% |

Real rooms clear more slowly because some air short-circuits between the inlet and outlet and misses the corners, so treat these as an upper bound. Higher ACH mainly buys speed: 8 ACH reaches in 15 minutes what 2 ACH takes a full hour to do.

## ACH50: measuring air leakage

Energy auditors use a blower door to depressurize a house to 50 pascals and measure the airflow needed to hold it. That airflow × 60 ÷ the house volume is ACH50, a measure of how leaky the building is. The 2021 International Energy Conservation Code requires new homes to test at no more than 3 ACH50 in most climate zones, and 5 in the warmest.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I calculate air changes per hour?

Multiply the airflow in CFM by 60 and divide by the room's volume in cubic feet. 120 CFM in a 1,440-cubic-foot room is 5 ACH.

### How many air changes per hour does a house need?

For fresh air, ASHRAE 62.2 sets ventilation in cfm, not ACH: about 90 cfm for a 2,000 sq ft, three-bedroom home, roughly 0.34 ACH.

### How many air changes per hour should an air purifier provide?

AHAM's two-thirds rule works out to about 5 ACH of filtered air at an 8-foot ceiling. Aim higher for allergies or smoke.

### What is ACH50?

The air changes per hour measured with a blower door at 50 pascals of pressure, used to rate how airtight a house is.

### How many air changes per hour clear smoke or a cooking smell?

Faster is better, and the exponential decay sets the pace: at 5 air changes an hour a well-mixed room is about 92% clear in 30 minutes, and at 8 ACH about 98%. A purifier only pulls out the smoke particles, so for cooking odors and gases you also need activated carbon or an exhaust fan venting outdoors. AHAM's two-thirds rule gives roughly 5 ACH of filtered air, and smoke is a case to aim higher.

### What ACH does a bedroom need?

Fresh-air codes set bedroom ventilation in CFM, not ACH, so there is no single required number. For an air purifier, AHAM's two-thirds rule works out to about 5 filtered air changes an hour at an 8-foot ceiling, and light sleepers with allergies often run more. A larger CADR reaches that target on a quieter fan setting, which matters most at night.
