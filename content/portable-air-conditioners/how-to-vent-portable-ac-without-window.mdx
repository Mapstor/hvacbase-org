# How to Vent a Portable AC Without a Window (5 Methods)

Your room has no standard window, or none where the portable AC needs to go, and you're trying to figure out how to vent it so it actually cools instead of just blowing hot air around. Maybe it's a room with only a sliding door, a basement, an office with a drop ceiling, or a casement window that doesn't work with the standard vent kit.

Good news: there are several proven ways to vent a portable AC without a normal window, and most are DIY-friendly. This guide walks through five methods, from easiest to most involved, with the tools you need, roughly what each costs, and, honestly, which ones work best and which have catches. The one rule you can't get around: the hot air has to go somewhere outside the room, or the AC won't cool.

**You can vent a portable AC without a window through a sliding glass door, a drop ceiling, a through-wall vent, an existing dryer vent, or a casement-window adapter.** The right choice depends on your space and whether you rent or own: renters usually want the removable options (sliding door, casement adapter), while homeowners can consider a permanent through-wall vent for the best performance.

## First, why venting isn't optional

Before the methods, one thing has to be clear: you cannot skip venting a portable AC in cooling mode. Here's the physics. A portable AC works by moving heat out of your room, and it exhausts that heat as hot air (typically 110°F to 140°F) through its hose. Every 1,000 watts the unit draws produces roughly 3,400 BTU of heat (about 3.4 BTU per watt), and all of it has to leave the room. If that hot exhaust stays inside, the net effect is *heating*, not cooling, you'd actually make the room warmer.

The only modes that work without venting are fan-only (which doesn't cool) and dehumidifier mode (which removes moisture but adds some heat). For real cooling, you need one of the five exhaust paths below.

## Method 1: Sliding glass door (the easiest)

If you have a sliding glass door, this is usually the simplest solution, and it's renter-friendly since it's fully removable. The idea is to fill the gap from a partially-opened door with a panel and route the exhaust hose through it.

You'll need a portable-AC sliding-door kit (a telescoping panel, roughly $30 to $60) or a DIY panel of plexiglass or rigid foam board (roughly $15 to $25), plus foam weatherstripping tape and a hose adapter if the kit doesn't include one. A commercial kit telescopes to your door height and has the hose hole built in; a DIY panel means cutting a 5 to 6 inch hole about two feet from the bottom and sealing all the edges with weatherstripping so hot air can't leak back in. Secure the panel with a door bar or dowel in the track.

The trade-offs: it's easy to install and remove with no permanent changes, but a thin foam or plastic panel is a **security weak point** in a door, so add a security bar or a piece of plywood cut to fit (heavier, but far more secure), and a thin panel may feel warm since it doesn't insulate perfectly. It reduces the door's usability while installed.

## Method 2: Drop ceiling (for offices and basements)

Common in offices, finished basements, and commercial spaces, a drop (suspended) ceiling can be a good venting path, because the space above the tiles, the plenum, usually connects to unconditioned space. You route the exhaust hose up through a modified ceiling tile.

You'll need a spare ceiling tile you can cut, a 5 to 6 inch vent collar or flange, and a length of duct to reach up into the plenum. Cut a hole in the tile for the collar, run the hose up through it, and make sure the hot air is actually going somewhere it can escape or dissipate.

The honest catch: **a drop-ceiling plenum isn't always truly "outside."** If the hot exhaust just pools above the tiles in a space that recirculates back into your building's air, you'll lose some cooling effectiveness (expect a modest efficiency loss versus a direct-to-outside vent). It works best where the plenum genuinely vents to unconditioned or exterior space. Check where that air actually ends up before committing.

## Method 3: Through-wall vent (best performance, permanent)

Drilling a dedicated vent through an exterior wall gives the best, most direct exhaust path, and it frees up your doors and windows entirely. But it's a permanent modification and the most involved option.

You'll need a wall vent cap with a damper (about $15 to $30), rigid or flexible duct, a core drill bit or large hole saw (5 to 6 inch, often rentable), a stud finder, and silicone caulk to seal it. The critical safety steps: **use a stud finder and be certain you're not drilling into electrical wiring, plumbing, or gas lines inside the wall**, locate the studs and utilities first, drill from inside angled slightly downward (so rain drains out), install the vented cap on the exterior, and seal thoroughly with caulk.

This is the best-performing method (direct exhaust, no efficiency loss), but it's a hard job, it's **not renter-friendly** (it needs landlord permission and usually isn't allowed for tenants), and if you're not confident locating what's inside your wall, this is worth hiring out, hitting a wire or pipe is a serious mistake. Renters should use Method 1, 2, or 5 instead.

## Method 4: Existing dryer vent (convenient, with a real caveat)

If your room is near the laundry, an existing dryer vent already provides a hole to the outside, so you can route the AC exhaust through it. You'll typically need a 5-to-4-inch duct reducer, since most dryer vents are 4 inches while portable-AC hoses are 5 inches.

Here's the honest catch that most guides skip: **a dryer vent isn't an ideal AC exhaust.** Two problems. First, the size mismatch, reducing a 5-inch hose down to a 4-inch dryer vent restricts airflow, which makes the AC work harder and lose some efficiency. Second, dryer vents usually have a **backdraft damper and often lint buildup**, both of which further restrict the airflow the AC needs. If you use this method, clean the vent line first, and don't run the dryer and AC through it at the same time. It's convenient if the vent is right there, but expect some efficiency loss, and don't count on it as your best option if another method is available.

## Method 5: Casement or crank window adapter (for non-standard windows)

Casement windows (the kind that crank open vertically) don't fit standard portable-AC window kits, which are built for horizontal sliding or double-hung windows. The solution is a casement-specific adapter panel, a tall, narrow panel sized for the vertical opening, either a commercial casement kit or a DIY panel cut to fit.

You'll cut or buy a panel to fill the vertical opening, add the hose hole, and seal the edges with weatherstripping. It's renter-friendly and removable, similar to the sliding-door method but oriented for a tall, narrow window. The main challenge is just getting a good seal on the taller opening, weatherstrip it well so hot air doesn't leak back in.

## Which method should you choose?

Quick guidance by situation:

| Your situation | Best method |
|----------------|-------------|
| Apartment with a sliding door or balcony | Sliding glass door (Method 1) |
| Office or basement with a drop ceiling | Drop ceiling (Method 2), if the plenum vents out |
| Homeowner wanting the best, permanent setup | Through-wall vent (Method 3) |
| Room right next to the laundry | Dryer vent (Method 4), with the caveats above |
| Room with a casement/crank window | Casement adapter (Method 5) |
| Renting | Methods 1, 2, or 5 (removable, no permanent changes) |

Whatever method you choose, two things matter most for actually cooling well: **keep the exhaust hose as short and straight as you can** (long, kinked, or coiled hoses trap heat and hurt performance), and **seal the opening well** so the hot air you just pushed out doesn't leak back in. And running a portable AC's hose into an attic or crawlspace can dump heat and moisture into those spaces, so vent to genuinely outside or well-ventilated unconditioned areas.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can you use a portable AC without venting it?**
Not for cooling. In cooling mode, a portable AC produces hot exhaust that must leave the room, if it doesn't, the room gets warmer, not cooler. Only fan mode (no cooling) and dehumidifier mode work unvented, and dehumidifier mode adds some heat.

**Can I vent a portable AC through a dryer vent?**
You can, but it's not ideal. Dryer vents are usually 4 inches (portable AC hoses are 5), so you need a reducer that restricts airflow, and the vent's backdraft damper and any lint buildup restrict it further, costing you some efficiency. Clean the vent first and don't run the dryer at the same time. It's convenient but not the best-performing option.

**How do I vent a portable AC in a room with no windows at all?**
Your options are a sliding glass door (if present), a drop ceiling that vents to unconditioned space, a permanent through-wall vent (best performance, homeowners only), or an existing dryer vent. Every method routes the hot exhaust out of the room somehow, that's the non-negotiable requirement.

**Does a longer exhaust hose reduce cooling?**
Yes. A longer, kinked, or coiled hose traps heat and increases back-pressure, making the AC work harder and cool less effectively. Keep the hose as short and straight as possible, and avoid extending it beyond the manufacturer's recommendation.

**Can I vent a portable AC into another room or a closet?**
No. Venting into another interior space (a closet, hallway, or adjacent room) just moves the heat inside your home, it doesn't remove it, so your space won't cool properly and you may create a hot, humid pocket. The exhaust has to reach outside or a genuinely unconditioned, well-ventilated space.
