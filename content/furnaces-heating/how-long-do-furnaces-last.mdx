# How Long Do Furnaces Last? (Lifespan by Type + 10 Replacement Signs)

A gas furnace lasts **15–25 years** with an average of 18–20 years. Electric furnaces last the longest at **20–30 years** (average 25) due to no combustion stress. Oil furnaces last **15–25 years** (average 18) but require diligent maintenance to reach the upper range. The component that typically determines end-of-life is the heat exchanger — once it cracks, the furnace is usually not worth repairing.

Here's how to estimate your furnace's remaining life, the 10 signs it needs replacing, and how to make the repair-vs-replace decision.

## 10 Signs Your Furnace Needs Replacing

1. **Age over 15–20 years.** Even if it's still running, a furnace past 15 years is living on borrowed time. Parts become harder to find, efficiency has degraded, and a major component failure is increasingly likely.

2. **Rising energy bills.** If your gas bills have increased 15–25% over the past few years despite stable rates and consistent thermostat settings, the furnace is losing efficiency due to internal wear.

3. **Frequent repairs.** If you've needed 3 or more repairs in the past 2 years, the system is in decline. Each repair patches one component while others continue aging.

4. **Yellow burner flame.** A healthy gas flame is blue. A yellow or orange flame indicates incomplete combustion, potentially from a degrading heat exchanger or burner assembly.

5. **Cracked heat exchanger.** This is the most definitive replacement signal. A cracked heat exchanger can leak carbon monoxide and costs $1,500–$3,500 to replace — often not worth it on an older furnace.

6. **Uneven heating.** If some rooms are too hot while others are cold, the furnace may be losing capacity, the blower may be weakening, or ductwork has degraded. While ductwork issues are separate, an aging furnace often contributes.

7. **Strange noises.** Banging, squealing, rattling, or popping sounds that weren't present before indicate worn components — bearings, belts, heat exchanger expansion, or loose parts.

8. **Excessive dust.** An aging furnace with failing seals and worn components can pull more dust into the system and distribute it through the house.

9. **Thermostat adjustments don't help.** If you keep raising the thermostat but the house still feels cold, the furnace is losing heating capacity.

10. **Carbon monoxide detector alarms.** Any CO alarm during furnace operation demands immediate inspection. On an older furnace, this often indicates a cracked heat exchanger warranting replacement.

## The Repair vs. Replace Decision

<Callout type="takeaway">
**Three decision rules:**

**50% Rule:** If the repair costs more than 50% of a new furnace AND the furnace is over 15 years old, replace it.

**$5,000 Rule:** Multiply age × repair cost. If the result exceeds $5,000, replace it. (Example: 16 years × $350 repair = $5,600 → replace.)

**3-Repair Rule:** If you've had 3+ repairs in 2 years, the system is in overall decline. Further repairs are postponing the inevitable.
</Callout>

## What Happens During a Heat Exchanger Inspection

A modern heat exchanger inspection combines a **direct visual scan** with **combustion analyzer readings** — either method alone can miss defects that the other catches. A qualified technician removes the burner assembly and inducer/flue collector, then feeds a slim borescope probe (typically 2–4 mm) into the primary heat exchanger passages. The live video reveals the internal chamber walls, seams, and bends where thermal-fatigue cracks most commonly form, and any soot streaking downstream of a defect.

The combustion analyzer half of the inspection is what catches cracks the camera cannot see. The technician samples flue gas for CO, O2, and stack temperature before and after cycling the blower on; a jump in CO in the supply-air register when the blower engages indicates flue gases are being pulled through a breach into the airstream, even when the borescope shows no obvious crack. ANSI Z21.47 and manufacturer service literature both treat this pressure-differential test as a primary indicator, not an optional add-on.

Older inspection methods — a mirror-and-flashlight visual through the burner ports, or the "salt shaker" test where a technician sprinkles salt into the burner compartment while the blower runs and listens for pings — are still used but have well-known limits. They typically miss hairline cracks below roughly 1 mm and often can't inspect the upper bends of a clamshell exchanger at all. A reputable inspection report should name the inspection method used, list the CO readings taken, and include borescope stills if a defect is called out — a verbal "looked cracked to me" isn't enough to justify a $1,500–$3,500 exchanger job or a full furnace replacement.

## Repair-vs-Replace: Dollar Thresholds by Age

The rules in the previous section (50% rule, $5,000 rule, 3-repair rule) give a fast yes/no, but the underlying math is more useful once you know what a replacement actually costs. Typical installed replacement pricing in most U.S. markets falls into two brackets: **$4,000–$8,000 for a basic 80% AFUE unit** and **$6,000–$12,000 for a 95%+ AFUE condensing unit** with the added PVC venting, condensate drain, and (in some jurisdictions) permit and combustion-air work. These are widely cited industry ranges — actual quotes swing with tonnage, ductwork modifications, and regional labor rates.

Hold those numbers against the repair quote and one clear threshold emerges: **any single repair over ~$1,500 on a furnace older than 15 years is almost always a replace call**, because the surrounding components (inducer, control board, blower motor, gas valve) are statistically close to their own end-of-life and each will trigger another service visit. The heat exchanger itself sits at the top of this ladder — a $1,500–$3,500 exchanger swap on a 16-year-old 80% AFUE furnace produces a repaired unit worth roughly the cost of a new one, with none of the efficiency gain or fresh warranty.

A useful framing: divide the repair cost by the *expected remaining life* of the furnace, not its age. A $900 inducer on a well-maintained 8-year-old unit with 10+ years likely left costs about $90/year of "kept life"; the same $900 on a 22-year-old unit realistically buys 1–3 more years and works out to $300–$900/year. The moment that per-year figure crosses the annual amortized cost of a new furnace, replacement wins on lifecycle cost.

## Real-World Age Guidelines

Rather than run every quote through a formula, most decisions can be resolved by age bracket:

- **0–10 years — repair, almost always.** The unit is typically still inside its parts warranty (most major brands cover parts 10 years with registration), and 5–15 years of useful life likely remain. Even a $1,000–$1,500 repair pencils out.
- **10–15 years — it depends on the repair.** Small repairs (flame sensor, igniter, capacitor) are obvious fixes. Larger repairs on an 80% AFUE furnace should be weighed against the efficiency upgrade to a 95%+ AFUE unit; the U.S. DOE notes that going from 80% to 95%+ typically cuts fuel use by roughly the difference in ratings, but the **payback on that upgrade alone is commonly cited at 5–8 years and varies heavily with climate and gas price** — cutting it close for a furnace already halfway through its life.
- **15–25 years — replacement usually wins.** Any repair above roughly $1,500 has poor economics because other components are approaching end-of-life. This bracket is also where heat-exchanger fatigue cracks become statistically more likely.
- **25+ years — replace regardless of the repair.** Parts availability drops sharply for discontinued platforms, combustion efficiency has degraded well below the nameplate AFUE, and the risk profile on the heat exchanger no longer justifies incremental spend.

Two caveats sit on top of these brackets. First, **any confirmed cracked heat exchanger at 15+ years is a replace call** — the earlier repair-cost math doesn't apply once a CO-leak path exists. Second, **replacing proactively in shoulder season** (spring or early fall) typically produces lower installed pricing and better contractor selection than an emergency swap in January, so a borderline 18-year-old furnace is often better replaced on your schedule than on its own.

## Key Takeaways

<Callout type="takeaway">
- **Heat exchanger cracking is what actually kills a furnace.** Replacement runs $1,500–$3,500 and rarely pencils out on an older unit — which is why electric (no combustion, no heat exchanger stress) outlasts gas and oil by 5–10 years on average.
- **Annual maintenance and regular filter changes** are the two most impactful things you can do to extend furnace life.
- **Proper sizing at installation** prevents short cycling, which is one of the top lifespan-shortening factors.
- **Start budgeting for replacement at year 15** — even if the furnace is still running.
- Use the **50% rule, $5,000 rule, and 3-repair rule** for objective repair-vs-replace decisions.
- **A cracked heat exchanger on a 15+ year furnace** is almost always a replace signal.
- **Proactive replacement** in the off-season saves money versus emergency replacement in January.
</Callout>
