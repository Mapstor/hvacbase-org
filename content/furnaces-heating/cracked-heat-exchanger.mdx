# Cracked Heat Exchanger

A furnace's heat exchanger is the metal chamber that keeps burning fuel and its exhaust separate from the air your blower sends through the house. A crack breaks that separation, which is why it's treated as a safety problem, not just a repair.

**A cracked heat exchanger can let exhaust, including carbon monoxide, mix into the air circulating through your home. Signs include soot around the burners, a burner flame that wavers or changes when the blower starts, a strange smell, or a carbon monoxide alarm. If you suspect one, turn the furnace off and have a qualified technician inspect it; if a CO alarm sounds, get everyone outside and call for help.**

## Why it's dangerous

Carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless. The CDC reports that more than 400 people in the U.S. die each year from unintentional carbon monoxide poisoning not linked to fires. A cracked heat exchanger is one way combustion gases can reach living spaces, which is why working CO alarms matter in any home with fuel-burning appliances; see the [carbon monoxide detector guide](/carbon-monoxide-detector-guide).

## Warning signs

- **A carbon monoxide alarm** going off, or symptoms such as headache, dizziness and nausea that ease when you leave the house.
- **Soot or rust** around the burners or on the heat exchanger.
- **Flame changes:** a burner flame that flickers, lifts or changes color when the blower comes on can mean air is entering through a crack.
- **An unusual smell** when the furnace runs.
- **Visible cracks or separation** a technician finds during an inspection.

## What causes cracks

The heat exchanger expands every time the furnace heats and contracts as it cools. Over many years, that cycling fatigues the metal.

Overheating speeds it up, most often from restricted airflow: a clogged filter, closed or blocked vents, or undersized ducts. An oversized furnace that short-cycles adds more heating and cooling cycles.

## How a technician confirms it

Inspection usually combines a visual check with a camera or mirror, a look at the flame as the blower starts, and a combustion analysis of the exhaust. Some cracks are hard to see, so a technician may test carbon monoxide in the supply air as well. Ask what the evidence is, and get a second opinion before replacing a furnace on a crack you haven't been shown.

## What a technician should show you

A crack is condemned on evidence, not on a verbal description. Before you agree to replace a furnace, ask the technician to show you the proof.

- **A photo or video** from an inspection camera showing the actual crack or separation, and where on the exchanger it sits.
- **A combustion-analyzer reading** of the exhaust, and where possible the supply air, with the carbon monoxide value recorded in parts per million.
- **The flame test demonstrated** live: whether the burner flame flickers, lifts or changes color when the blower starts.

No image and no carbon monoxide measurement is a reason to get a second opinion before spending anything. Be wary of a free inspection that ends in a pressured, same-day replacement quote.

## What to do

1. **Turn the furnace off** if a crack is suspected, and use safe temporary heat.
2. **If a CO alarm sounds or anyone feels ill**, get everyone outside and call 911 or your gas utility from outside.
3. **Have it inspected** by a qualified technician.
4. **Check the warranty:** heat exchangers often carry longer manufacturer warranties than other parts.
5. **Decide repair or replacement** with the technician; on an older furnace, replacement is common.

## Repair or replace

Two fixes exist: replacing just the heat exchanger, or replacing the whole furnace. Swapping the exchanger is labor-intensive because the furnace has to be largely taken apart to reach it.

Warranty shapes the choice. Many manufacturers warranty the heat exchanger longer than other parts, sometimes for as long as the original owner keeps the furnace. A warranty usually covers the part itself and not the labor to install it, so confirm what yours includes before deciding.

Age usually settles it. ENERGY STAR suggests considering replacement once a furnace is more than 15 years old, so a cracked exchanger on a furnace near or past that mark often makes a new furnace the better value; see [how long HVAC systems last](/hvac-system-lifespan).

Replacing an old furnace also captures efficiency. A standard furnace runs around 80% AFUE with a metal flue, while a condensing model reaches 90% or more by condensing its own exhaust, so more of the fuel becomes heat. The [furnace guide](/furnace-guide) covers that choice.

## Preventing it

Change filters on schedule and keep vents open so the furnace never overheats, and have it inspected before each heating season; see [how often to change your HVAC filter](/how-often-change-hvac-filter).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a cracked heat exchanger dangerous?

It can be, because it can let exhaust, including carbon monoxide, into the air circulating through your home. Turn the furnace off and have it inspected.

### What are the signs of a cracked heat exchanger?

Soot around the burners, a flame that changes when the blower starts, an unusual smell, or a carbon monoxide alarm.

### Can a cracked heat exchanger be repaired?

Usually the heat exchanger or the furnace is replaced rather than repaired. Check the manufacturer's warranty first.

### What causes a heat exchanger to crack?

Years of heating and cooling cycles, sped up by overheating from restricted airflow, such as dirty filters or blocked vents.

### How long can I keep running the furnace while I wait for a repair?

Treat a suspected crack as a reason to stop using the furnace, not to keep it running on borrowed time. If you need heat in the meantime, use safe alternatives and make sure working carbon monoxide alarms are on every level and near where people sleep. Any alarm or any symptoms means everyone goes outside and you call for help.

### Does a carbon monoxide alarm alone prove the heat exchanger is cracked?

No. An alarm tells you carbon monoxide is present, not where it is coming from. A blocked flue, a water heater, a car running in an attached garage, or another fuel-burning appliance can all trigger it, so the source still has to be found by inspection.

### Should I replace the whole furnace or just the heat exchanger?

It depends mostly on the furnace's age and warranty. ENERGY STAR suggests considering replacement once a furnace is more than 15 years old, and swapping the exchanger is labor-intensive, so on an older unit a new furnace is often the better value. On a newer furnace still under heat-exchanger warranty, replacing the part can make sense.
