# Electrical Panel Upgrade Cost: 100 to 200 Amp

You're planning an upgrade that needs more electrical capacity, maybe a heat pump, an EV charger, or a whole-home electrification project, and your existing panel can't handle it. Or an electrician told you your 100-amp panel needs to become a 200-amp panel and quoted a number that made you wince. This guide breaks down what a panel upgrade actually costs and why.

The figures are rough estimates in 2023 dollars, and costs vary significantly by your home's situation and region, so always get itemized quotes. One thing to be clear about up front: **a panel upgrade is licensed-electrician work requiring a permit and inspection, never a DIY project**, the service entrance carries lethal voltage and the work is safety-critical.

**Replacing a 200-amp electrical panel costs about $1,000 to $3,100 installed (about $1,500 at the mid estimate), per NREL's REMDB, which rates this data roughly.** A simple panel swap, when your existing wiring already supports 200A, sits at the low end; a full service upgrade that also involves the utility, meter base and service-entrance cable costs more. The full breakdown follows.

## Panel upgrade cost summary

The type of upgrade you need is the biggest cost factor:

| Panel replacement | Low | Mid | High |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100-amp panel | about $500 | about $750 | about $1,050 |
| 200-amp panel | about $1,000 | about $1,500 | about $3,100 |

NREL rates this data as having no clear sources, so treat it as rough and approximate. A full service upgrade that also replaces the meter base, service-entrance cable or service drop costs more than a bare panel swap. A smart panel with load management, or stepping up to 320-amp or 400-amp service, costs more still; which one you need depends on what's already in place, which the "types of upgrades" section below explains.

## What you're paying for

A panel upgrade quote covers several components. The REMDB figure already bundles labor and removal of the old panel, so these are what a quote itemizes rather than separate line prices:

- **The 200A main breaker panel** itself.
- **Breakers** (transferred from the old panel plus any new ones).
- **200A meter socket** (if it needs replacing).
- **Service entrance cable** (the heavy wire from meter to panel).
- **Weatherhead and mast** (where the service enters).
- **Grounding electrode system upgrade** (to meet current code).
- **Permit and inspection.**
- **Utility coordination** (if meter or service work is involved).

### Labor cost varies a lot by region

Labor is a big part of the total, and electrician rates vary widely by location. The physical work typically takes 6 to 12 hours. Urban coastal metros such as New York, Boston and the West Coast cities run the highest, while rural areas of the South and Midwest run the lowest.

## The types of upgrades explained

Not every "panel upgrade" is the same job. Which type you need drives the cost:

- **Type 1, panel swap only:** your service entrance wiring (meter to panel) is already rated for 200A, so only the panel itself is replaced, the roughly $1,000 to $3,100 REMDB range above. Common in homes built after ~1990 that have a 100A panel on 200A-capable wiring.
- **Type 2, panel + meter socket:** the panel and the meter socket both need replacing, but the service cable is fine.
- **Type 3, full service upgrade:** the entire service entrance, from the utility connection down to the panel, needs upgrading. Common in homes built before 1980 with original electrical.
- **Type 4, full upgrade + service drop:** everything in Type 3 plus the overhead or underground service line from the utility (the "service drop") also needs replacing.
- **Type 5, smart panel:** a panel with built-in load management that can monitor and control circuits.

### Smart panels: when they make sense

A **smart panel** dynamically manages your home's loads, and it can sometimes avoid a much more expensive service upgrade. If your calculated load exceeds 200A, instead of paying much more than a 200-amp panel replacement for 320A service, a smart panel can intelligently prioritize circuits, for example, reducing EV charging while the AC is running so the main breaker doesn't trip.

For an all-electric home flirting with its capacity limit, that load management can be cheaper than upsizing the whole service. Whether it's worth it depends on your specific loads and how close you are to your limit.

## Do you actually need an upgrade?

Before assuming you need a bigger panel, check: often you don't. **Have an electrician perform an NEC Article 220 load calculation**, which adds up your home's electrical demand to determine the service size you need. Rough guidance:

| Home profile | Typical calculated load | Likely service need |
|---|---|---|
| Small home, gas heat/appliances | 60–100A | 100A often adequate |
| Average home, some electric | 100–150A | 150–200A |
| All-electric home | 150–200A | 200A |
| All-electric + EV charger | 175–225A | 200A (or smart panel) |
| All-electric + 2 EVs + hot tub | 200–280A | 320A or smart panel |

### Warning signs you need more capacity

Some signs point to a genuine capacity problem (as opposed to a specific fault):

- **Breakers trip frequently** when running multiple appliances.
- The **panel is warm to the touch**, a sign of overloaded connections and a fire hazard, get this checked promptly.
- You're **out of open breaker slots** and need to add circuits.
- You still have a **fuse box** or an old panel from a brand with known safety recalls.
- Lights **dim** when large appliances start.

If you see the "warm panel" sign especially, don't wait, that's a potential fire hazard that needs a licensed electrician.

## What's involved: the process

A panel upgrade is a multi-step job, usually spread over a few weeks (utility coordination is often the bottleneck):

1. **Assessment and permit:** an electrician evaluates your system, proposes the scope, and files the permit with your local building department. Utility notification happens here if meter work is involved.
2. **Installation:** the utility disconnects power (scheduled in advance), the electrician swaps the panel and any service components, transfers the circuits, and upgrades the grounding.
3. **Inspection and reconnection:** the local inspector must approve the work before the utility reconnects power.

**Timeline: typically 1 to 4 weeks**, with utility scheduling usually the longest part.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much does it cost to upgrade an electrical panel?**
Replacing a 200-amp panel costs about $1,000 to $3,100 (about $1,500 at the mid estimate), per NREL's REMDB, which rates this data roughly. A simple panel swap, when the existing wiring is already 200A-capable, is cheapest; a full service upgrade with a new meter and cable, or 320A/400A service for large all-electric homes, costs more. Get itemized quotes for your situation.

**Do I need a permit to upgrade my electrical panel?**
Yes, all jurisdictions require a permit for a panel upgrade, because it changes the service entrance, a major electrical modification. The electrician typically pulls the permit, and an inspection is required before the utility will reconnect power. This is not a DIY job, both for safety and because unpermitted service work won't pass inspection.

**Why does a heat pump or EV charger need a panel upgrade?**
These are large electrical loads. A heat pump draws a substantial dedicated circuit, and an EV charger can pull 40 to 50+ amps continuously. If your existing panel is near its capacity (common with older 100A service), adding one of these can exceed what the panel can safely supply, so an NEC Article 220 load calculation determines whether you need more capacity.

**Can I avoid a panel upgrade?**
Sometimes. A load calculation may show your existing service is adequate.

If you're just over capacity, a smart panel with load management can sometimes avoid a costly service upsizing by intelligently prioritizing circuits. And some specific additions can be handled with circuit changes rather than a full upgrade, an electrician can advise.

**How long does a panel upgrade take?**
The physical work is usually done in a day (6 to 12 hours), but the full process typically spans 1 to 4 weeks including permitting, scheduling the utility disconnect and reconnect, and inspection. Utility coordination is usually the bottleneck.

## Where these numbers come from

The installed-cost figures come from NREL's National Residential Efficiency Measures Database (REMDB), 2024 release, which models a retrofit replacement in 2023 dollars and gives a low, mid and high estimate. They are total installed costs, including removing the old panel and before any rebates, so prices have generally risen since 2023. NREL rates the panel data as having no clear sources, so treat it as rough; work REMDB does not price, such as the meter socket, service-entrance cable, service drop, grounding, permits and utility coordination, has no figure here and is described as a cost driver instead.

The load-calculation method (adding up a home's electrical demand to determine required service size) follows **NEC Article 220** of the National Electrical Code (NFPA 70).

A panel upgrade is safety-critical work on the service entrance that typically requires a licensed electrician, a permit and an inspection. The scope of work and a proper load calculation matter more than any particular panel brand.
