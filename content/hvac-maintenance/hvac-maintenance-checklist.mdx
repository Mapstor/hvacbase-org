# HVAC Maintenance Checklist: Seasonal DIY Guide

You want your HVAC system to just work, without a surprise breakdown on the hottest day of summer or a repair bill you didn't see coming. The nagging question is: **what do I actually need to do to keep it running, what can I handle myself, and what needs a pro?** Most people either do nothing until something breaks, or overpay for maintenance they don't need.

Here's the honest, practical checklist, split into what you can do yourself and what genuinely needs a technician, based on the actual maintenance guidance from ENERGY STAR and the Department of Energy. Keep up with a handful of simple habits and schedule the right professional check-ups, and you'll avoid most breakdowns and keep your energy bills down. The single most important habit takes **30 seconds a month**.

**The core of HVAC maintenance is simple: check your filter monthly, keep the outdoor unit clear, and get a professional tune-up once a year (AC in spring, heating in fall).** ENERGY STAR recommends those annual pre-season check-ups, and the DIY tasks below fill the gaps between them. Everything involving refrigerant, electrical, or sealed components should be left to a licensed technician.

## The DIY checklist (what you can do yourself)

These are safe, effective, and cheap. None of them require special tools or expertise.

**Every month:**
- **Check your air filter.** This is the single highest-return maintenance task. A clogged filter restricts airflow, makes the system work harder, and per the DOE can increase energy use by 5–15%. Check it monthly and change a standard filter every 1–3 months (more with pets or allergies). Full details in our [filter-change guide](/how-often-change-hvac-filter).

**Seasonally (spring and fall):**
- **Clear the outdoor unit.** Remove leaves, grass clippings, and debris from around the condenser, and keep plants and shrubs trimmed back at least 2 feet so air can flow freely. A blocked condenser makes the system work harder.
- **Gently clean the outdoor coil.** With the power off, hose off pollen and dirt from the outside of the condenser coil (from the inside out if you can access it). Don't use a pressure washer, the fins bend easily.
- **Clear the condensate drain.** The drain line (a PVC pipe near your indoor unit) removes the moisture your AC pulls from the air, and it clogs with algae over time. Pouring a cup of white vinegar down it periodically helps prevent clogs that can cause water damage or shut the system down.
- **Check your thermostat settings.** Make sure the schedule still matches your routine. Per the DOE, setting it back when you're away (and using programmable schedules) meaningfully cuts cooling and heating costs.
- **Make sure vents and registers are open and unblocked** by furniture or rugs, blocked airflow strains the system.

**Once a year (a good habit):**
- **Replace the thermostat batteries** (if it uses them) so it doesn't die at an inconvenient time.

## The professional checklist (what needs a technician)

This is where ENERGY STAR's guidance matters most. A proper annual tune-up includes tasks that require training, tools, and, for refrigerant, legal certification. According to **ENERGY STAR's maintenance checklist**, a professional pre-season tune-up should include:

- **Checking and adjusting the refrigerant level.** Too much or too little refrigerant makes the system less efficient and shortens equipment life. Handling refrigerant is federally regulated (EPA Section 608) and requires certification, this is not a DIY task.
- **Tightening all electrical connections and measuring voltage and current on motors.** Faulty electrical connections are an unsafe-operation and component-life risk.
- **Cleaning the evaporator and condenser coils thoroughly.** Dirty coils are a leading cause of inefficiency and system failure.
- **Checking the thermostat calibration** and system controls.
- **Inspecting the condensate drain and pan.**
- **Testing system startup, operation, and shutoff.**

**For gas furnaces, one professional task is a genuine safety must:** a **heat exchanger inspection.** A cracked heat exchanger can leak carbon monoxide into your home, which is why this needs a licensed technician's eyes before each heating season. (This is also why you should have working [CO detectors](/carbon-monoxide-detector-guide) regardless.) Don't defer this one.

## When to schedule (and why timing matters)

**ENERGY STAR's advice: check the cooling system in spring and the heating system in fall**, before you actually need each one. The reasoning is practical, contractors get slammed once summer heat and winter cold arrive, so scheduling in the shoulder seasons means you're not trying to book an emergency visit during the first heat wave. A memorable trick ENERGY STAR suggests: tie your check-ups to the spring and fall time changes so you don't forget.

A note on "maintenance plans": many contractors offer annual plans (typically covering two visits plus perks like priority service and parts discounts). Whether one is worth it depends on the price and what's included, the core value is the two annual tune-ups. You don't need a plan to get maintenance; you can simply book the spring and fall visits directly.

## The warning signs that mean "call now" (don't wait for the next tune-up)

Between scheduled visits, these symptoms mean your system needs attention sooner:

- **Higher bills with no change in usage** (something's lost efficiency).
- **Weak airflow** at the registers.
- **Ice on the outdoor unit** (beyond a normal brief defrost cycle in winter).
- **Short-cycling** (turning on and off rapidly).
- **Grinding, banging, or hissing noises** (hissing can mean a refrigerant leak).
- **Musty or burning smells** from the vents (musty = possible mold in the drain pan; burning = possible electrical issue, turn the system off).
- **Uneven temperatures** room to room.

Any one of these is worth a service call. Several of them (weak airflow, ice, short-cycling) often trace back to something as simple as a dirty filter, so check that first, but if the filter's clean and the symptom persists, get it looked at. For a system that runs but won't cool, our [AC not cooling guide](/ac-not-cooling) walks through the diagnosis.

## Does maintenance actually pay off?

Yes, and it's one of the better returns in home upkeep. A well-maintained system runs more efficiently (a dirty filter or coils alone can cost you 5–15% more in energy, per the DOE), breaks down less often, and lasts longer, delaying an expensive replacement. The math strongly favors keeping up: the cost of a filter and an annual tune-up is small next to an emergency repair or a system that dies years early. The best approach is the simplest: build the monthly filter check into your routine, book the two seasonal tune-ups, and handle the easy seasonal tasks yourself.

## Frequently asked questions

**How often should HVAC maintenance be done?**
Do the monthly filter check yourself, and schedule a professional tune-up once a year at minimum, ideally twice: cooling in spring, heating in fall (per ENERGY STAR). The seasonal DIY tasks (clearing the outdoor unit, the condensate drain, etc.) fit around those.

**What can I do myself vs. what needs a professional?**
You can handle filter changes, clearing and gently cleaning the outdoor unit, the condensate drain, thermostat settings, and keeping vents open. Leave refrigerant (federally regulated), electrical connections, gas/heat-exchanger inspection, and sealed-system repairs to a licensed technician, these involve safety hazards and specialized tools.

**When should I get an HVAC tune-up?**
In spring for your air conditioner and fall for your furnace or heat pump, before peak season. ENERGY STAR recommends these pre-season check-ups, and scheduling in the shoulder seasons is cheaper and easier than booking during a heat wave or cold snap.

**Is an HVAC maintenance plan worth it?**
It depends on the price and what's included. The core value is the two annual tune-ups, which you can also just book directly without a plan. Plans add perks like priority service and parts discounts; whether that's worth the cost is up to you.

**Why is a heat exchanger inspection important?**
A cracked heat exchanger in a gas furnace can leak carbon monoxide into your home, a serious safety risk. It requires a licensed technician to inspect, ideally before each heating season. This is one professional task you shouldn't skip, and it's a reason to always have working CO detectors.
