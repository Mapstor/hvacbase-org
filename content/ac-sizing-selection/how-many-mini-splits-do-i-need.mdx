# How Many Mini Splits Do I Need?

A mini split's indoor head heats and cools the space it's in, and not much beyond it. Air doesn't travel well through doorways and down hallways, so the number of heads you need follows the layout of your home more than its total square footage.

**Plan on one indoor head for each room you close off or want to control separately, plus one for each large open area. A three-bedroom home with an open living and kitchen area typically needs four or five heads, served by one multi-zone outdoor unit or several single-zone systems.**

## What counts as a zone

- **A closed room**, such as a bedroom or office, is its own zone. A head in the hallway won't keep a bedroom with a closed door comfortable.
- **An open area**, such as a combined living room, dining room and kitchen, can be one zone if one head can reach it, or two if it's long or L-shaped.
- **Small rooms with little load**, such as bathrooms, laundry rooms and closets, usually go without a head.

## Sizing each head

Size every head for its own room, not the house. ENERGY STAR's room air conditioner chart is a useful reference for a single room's cooling:

| Room area | Cooling capacity |
|---|---|
| 100 to 150 sq ft | 5,000 BTU/hr |
| 150 to 250 sq ft | 6,000 BTU/hr |
| 250 to 300 sq ft | 7,000 BTU/hr |
| 300 to 350 sq ft | 8,000 BTU/hr |
| 350 to 400 sq ft | 9,000 BTU/hr |
| 400 to 450 sq ft | 10,000 BTU/hr |
| 450 to 550 sq ft | 12,000 BTU/hr |
| 550 to 700 sq ft | 14,000 BTU/hr |
| 700 to 1,000 sq ft | 18,000 BTU/hr |

ENERGY STAR adjusts these for conditions: 10% less for a heavily shaded room, 10% more for a very sunny one, 600 BTU per hour for each regular occupant beyond two, and 4,000 more for a kitchen. Our [mini split sizing calculator](/mini-split-sizing-calculator) does this per room, and for heating as well.

## A two-bedroom condo, room by room

Square footage alone does not set the count; the room layout does. Take an 1,100 sq ft two-bedroom condo with an open living, dining and kitchen area and two bedrooms off a short hall.

Working through it room by room, using the ENERGY STAR chart above, gives three heads:

| Room | Area | Chart capacity | Its own zone? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open living/dining/kitchen | ~500 sq ft | 12,000 BTU/hr, +4,000 for the kitchen = 16,000 | Yes, one head (two if it is long or L-shaped) |
| Primary bedroom | ~200 sq ft | 6,000 BTU/hr | Yes |
| Second bedroom | ~160 sq ft | 6,000 BTU/hr | Yes |
| Bathroom | ~50 sq ft | none | No, too little load |
| Entry hall and closets | small | none | No |

Adding the three heads gives 16,000 + 6,000 + 6,000 = 28,000 BTU/hr, which is 28,000 ÷ 12,000 = about 2.3 tons of indoor capacity. The outdoor unit is sized below that sum, because the bedrooms and the living area rarely hit their peak load at the same moment.

## One multi-zone outdoor unit or several single-zone systems?

- **Multi-zone:** one outdoor unit feeds several indoor heads. It takes one spot outside and one electrical circuit, but if it fails, every zone loses heating and cooling.
- **Single-zone:** each head has its own outdoor unit. More equipment outside, but each system works independently and is matched to its room, which often gives higher efficiency ratings.

Many homes mix both: a single-zone system for the main living area and a multi-zone unit for the bedrooms.

## How many heads one outdoor unit can carry

A multi-zone outdoor unit connects to each indoor head through its own pair of refrigerant lines. The manufacturer publishes a combination table that lists which head sizes a given outdoor unit accepts, and your installer selects from it.

Two limits set the count. The outdoor unit has a fixed number of connection ports, and its rated capacity caps the total load the attached heads can draw.

Because rooms rarely peak together, the outdoor unit is rated for the diversified load, not the arithmetic sum of the heads. In the condo above, three heads total 28,000 BTU/hr on paper, yet a smaller outdoor unit from the combination table usually covers them.

## Grouping rooms under one ducted air handler

A concealed ducted air handler, sometimes called a slim-duct unit, hides above a ceiling or in a closet and feeds two or three nearby rooms through short ducts. It replaces several wall heads with one appliance and a few grilles.

The payoff is fewer heads and less wall clutter. Three 150 to 250 sq ft bedrooms would each call for a 6,000 BTU/hr head on the chart above, 18,000 in all; one ducted handler sized to their combined load can serve all three instead.

Grouping also lowers the number of heads the outdoor unit has to carry. For how the head count changes the installed price, see our [mini split installation cost](/mini-split-installation-cost) guide.

## Two-story and multi-floor homes

Warm air rises, so in summer an upstairs floor gains heat from the roof and from the level below, and in winter the upstairs runs warmer than the main floor. A single head cannot push conditioned air up or down a stairwell against that drift.

Plan on at least one zone per floor, and a head for each closed bedroom as you would on a single level. A stairwell open to both floors does not let one head serve both; it mostly lets the upstairs collect heat.

One head per floor is enough only for a small, open, stacked layout with few interior doors. A typical two-story home with bedrooms upstairs needs a head in each of them plus one for the main living area.

## Common mistakes

- **Oversizing heads.** A head much bigger than its room's load cycles on and off, which wastes energy and removes less humidity.
- **One big head for several rooms.** It overcools the room it's in and leaves the others warm.
- **Ignoring heating.** In cold climates, size for the heating load too; see [heat pumps in cold weather](/heat-pump-in-cold-weather).

## Adding mini splits to a home with central air

A single mini split is a common fix for a room the central system doesn't reach well, such as a bonus room over the garage, an addition or a sunroom. Size it for that room alone.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many mini split heads do I need for a 3-bedroom house?

Typically four or five: one for each bedroom and one or two for the living areas, depending on how open the layout is.

### Can one mini split cool a whole house?

Only a small, open one. In a house with closed-off rooms, one head can't move air through the doorways well enough.

### How many BTU do I need per room?

Use ENERGY STAR's chart above as a starting point: about 6,000 BTU per hour for a 150 to 250 sq ft bedroom, adjusted for sun, occupants and kitchens.

### Is a multi-zone or single-zone mini split better?

Single-zone systems work independently and are matched to one room; multi-zone saves outdoor space. The right choice depends on how many rooms you have and where the outdoor unit can go.

### How many mini splits do I need for a 1,500 sq ft house?

Count rooms, not area. A single-story 1,500 sq ft home with an open living area and two or three bedrooms usually needs three or four heads, since each closed bedroom is its own zone and the open area takes one or two.

### How many mini splits for a two-story house?

Plan on at least one zone per floor, plus a head for each closed bedroom. A single head cannot condition both levels through a stairwell, so even a small two-story home generally needs three or more heads.

### How many mini splits for an apartment or condo?

A 1,000 to 1,200 sq ft two-bedroom condo typically needs three heads: one for the open living and kitchen area and one for each bedroom. Bathrooms, closets and short hallways are skipped.
