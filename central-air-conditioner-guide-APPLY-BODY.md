# Central Air Conditioning

Central air cools the whole house from one system: an outdoor unit, a coil indoors, and ductwork that carries cooled air to every room. Getting the most from it comes down to three decisions, its size, its efficiency and the condition of your ducts.

**Size it by tonnage from a load calculation, choose an efficiency (SEER2) that suits your climate's cooling hours, and fix leaky ducts first. A 3-ton system running 1,500 full-load hours costs about $680 a year at 14.3 SEER2 and 18 cents per kWh, or $486 at 20 SEER2; the DOE puts a central air conditioner's lifespan at 15 to 20 years.**

## How central air works

The outdoor unit holds the compressor and a coil that releases heat outside. Refrigerant carries heat from the indoor coil, mounted on the furnace or air handler, to the outdoor unit. The blower pushes house air across the cold indoor coil and through the supply ducts, and return ducts bring it back.

## Sizing

Central systems are sized in tons; one ton is 12,000 BTU per hour. The industry method is ACCA's Manual J, a room-by-room load calculation.

An oversized system cools quickly but shuts off before removing much humidity; an undersized one runs nonstop on hot days. Our [AC tonnage calculator](/ac-tonnage-calculator) gives a starting estimate.

## Efficiency and running cost

SEER2 rates seasonal cooling efficiency. The federal minimum for split systems is 13.4 SEER2 in the North and 14.3 in the South and Southwest for systems under 45,000 BTU per hour. For a 3-ton system at 1,500 full-load hours and 18 cents per kWh:

| SEER2 | Cost per year |
|---|---|
| 13.4 | $725 |
| 14.3 | $680 |
| 16 | $608 |
| 20 | $486 |

Each step up saves less than the last; our [SEER2 comparison calculator](/seer2-comparison-calculator) works out whether a higher rating pays back for you.

## Installation cost

Installed prices depend on size, efficiency, ductwork condition and region. See our [central AC cost guide](/central-ac-cost-to-install) for typical ranges, and get at least three itemized quotes.

## Ducts

The DOE puts duct air losses at about 30% of a cooling system's energy, so a new system on leaky ducts won't deliver its rated efficiency. Have ducts checked and sealed when replacing equipment; see [duct leakage testing](/duct-leakage-testing).

## Refrigerants

New systems use lower-impact refrigerants, mainly R-454B and R-32, under the EPA's phase-down of R-410A. See the [refrigerant phase-out](/hvac-refrigerant-phase-out) for the dates and what they mean for repairs.

## Maintenance and lifespan

The DOE says clean filters can lower an air conditioner's energy use by 5 to 15%; change or clean them on schedule, and keep the outdoor unit clear. A pre-season tune-up catches refrigerant and electrical problems early; see [HVAC maintenance cost](/hvac-maintenance-cost). The DOE puts a central air conditioner's life at 15 to 20 years.

## Central air vs. heat pump vs. mini split

A heat pump is a central air conditioner that also heats. A ductless mini split avoids duct losses and cools room by room. See [mini split vs. central air](/mini-split-vs-central-air) and [furnace vs. heat pump](/furnace-vs-heat-pump).

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does central air last?

15 to 20 years, according to the DOE, with regular maintenance.

### What SEER2 rating should I buy?

At least the federal minimum for your region; higher ratings pay back faster where summers are long and hot. Compare the price difference with the yearly savings.

### What size central air do I need?

Use a Manual J load calculation; our tonnage calculator gives a starting estimate from your home's size, climate and insulation.

### Why isn't my central air cooling?

Common causes include a dirty filter, a blocked outdoor unit, low refrigerant and leaky ducts; see [AC not cooling](/ac-not-cooling).
