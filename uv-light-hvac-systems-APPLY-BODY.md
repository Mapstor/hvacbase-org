# UV Lights for HVAC Systems

UV lights in an HVAC system use ultraviolet-C light to damage the DNA of microorganisms. They're sold for two different jobs, keeping the cooling coil clean and disinfecting the air passing through, and the evidence for each is very different.

**UV-C lamps aimed at the cooling coil and drain pan can keep mold and bacterial growth off those always-damp surfaces. Disinfecting moving air is harder: the EPA notes that the brief exposure as air passes a lamp may not be enough to kill most bacteria and mold spores. Choose lamps that don't produce ozone, and treat UV as an add-on to good filtration, not a replacement.**

## Two kinds of HVAC UV systems

- **Coil and drain-pan irradiation:** lamps mounted near the indoor coil shine continuously on the coil and pan, which are damp whenever the system cools. Constant exposure over hours suits slow-growing films of mold and bacteria.
- **Air-stream disinfection:** lamps in the ductwork treat air as it flows past. Air moves quickly, so each particle gets only a fraction of a second of exposure.

## What the evidence says

Killing a microorganism takes a certain UV dose: light intensity times exposure time. On a stationary coil, exposure accumulates and the dose is easy to reach. In moving air, the EPA's guide to air cleaners notes that typical residential UV systems may not provide enough exposure to kill most bacteria and mold spores, though they can help keep surfaces such as coils from growing them.

## Ozone and safety

- **Ozone:** some UV lamps emit shorter wavelengths that create ozone, a lung irritant. The EPA warns against devices that produce ozone indoors. Choose lamps labeled ozone-free.
- **Eyes and skin:** UV-C can injure eyes and skin. Lamps should be inside the equipment, with interlocks or switches that turn them off when panels are opened.
- **Materials:** UV-C can degrade some plastics and insulation near the lamp over time; follow the installation instructions.

## Maintenance

UV-C lamps lose output with use even while they still glow, so they need replacing on the maker's schedule, often about yearly. Dust on the lamp blocks light, so clean it when you change it.

## When UV is worth considering

- A cooling coil that keeps growing mold or slime, or a recurring musty smell from the system.
- Very humid climates where the coil stays wet for long periods.

For cleaner air overall, a good filter matters more: see the [MERV rating chart](/merv-rating-chart) and [how to improve indoor air quality](/how-to-improve-indoor-air-quality). For a dirty coil, cleaning it is the first step; see [how to clean AC coils](/how-to-clean-ac-coils).

## Frequently asked questions

### Do UV lights in HVAC systems work?

For keeping the cooling coil and drain pan free of mold and bacteria, they can. For disinfecting moving air, the EPA notes that typical residential systems may not give enough exposure to kill most microorganisms.

### Do HVAC UV lights produce ozone?

Some do. Choose lamps labeled ozone-free; the EPA advises against ozone-producing devices indoors.

### How often do UV lights need replacing?

On the manufacturer's schedule, often about once a year, because UV-C output fades before the lamp stops glowing.

### Is a UV light better than a better air filter?

No. They do different jobs; filters capture particles, and a UV light mainly keeps the coil clean.
